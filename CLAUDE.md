# Ancient Trees: project instructions

## What this project is

Ancient Trees is a discovery platform that maps the most remarkable ancient trees of the world's great cities, up to ten per city and never padded. The owner is Hidde, a designer and tree enthusiast based in the Netherlands. The system researches, verifies and publishes; readers correct. Hidde sets direction, he is not the quality gate.

The end product: a website (ancienttrees.app) with an interactive map and one SEO page per city ("10 Most Beautiful Ancient Trees in [City]"), later followed by an iOS app with a freemium model. Where the paid line falls is undecided and Hidde's alone: see "The interim paywall line is still undecided" below, and hard rule 2.

## The value proposition

This is the core, and everything else serves it. Written 2026-07-24 by Hidde because the proposition had been drifting toward SEO and coverage, which are how people arrive, not why they stay. When anything here conflicts with an SEO or coverage instinct elsewhere, this wins.

**Ancient Trees is for people who love being outdoors and love trees.** The promise is simple: wherever you are, it shows you the remarkable old trees near you, gives you a short walk that strings a few together and tells you why each is worth seeing, and lets you collect the ones you have stood in front of. The feeling it sells is a good afternoon outside, looking up at something epic and old.

The one-sentence north star: *I am somewhere, I open it, it knows where I am, it shows me a cool tree nearby and a walk past a few, tells me why they are special, and I tick off the ones I visit.*

**The product is four verbs, settled with Hidde on 2026-07-26: find, walk, collect, season.** Find cool trees near you; walk a route past them; collect them with badges and points as the game; see them at their best with the season radar. Features outside these four (trip planners, audio guides, personal records, standalone themed routes) are parked ideas, not the product, and are not built without his explicit yes.

What this means for how we build:
- **The map and "trees near me" are the product.** They lead everywhere, the homepage first.
- **Cities, species and collections are acquisition, not the point.** They are SEO landing pages that bring people in from search. Keep them, they work, but they are the front door, not the house. On the homepage they sit below the value proposition, never in place of it.
- **Collections stay** (for example "the ten most remarkable trees of Europe"). A real SEO strategy, kept and on the backlog as such, secondary to the core. Not to be deleted.
- **The destination is: see the trees around you, walk them, collect them.** Accounts and an app are the vehicle. On 2026-07-26 Hidde opened the account track early, deliberately: build the app's collect-and-account backend now and wear it on the web, so the thinking and infrastructure exist before iOS. The conditions and current state live in "Where this is going" below.
- **Nothing is premium for now, but premium is the destination, and copy must never promise otherwise.** "Free today" is a growth tactic while we win users; the recorded end state is an account base with a premium tier and eventually an app (see "Where this is going"). So the site never says "free forever", "no accounts" or "always" anything: it simply does not gate today. Claims that survive the paywall (every tree free to explore, no ads, no tracking) are fine; claims the paywall would break are not. This distinction exists because a run once turned the sequencing into identity copy, and Hidde caught it.

When a page, a feature or a line of copy does not serve "have a good afternoon outside seeing epic trees near you," it is decoration or acquisition, and it must not crowd out the core.

## Repository structure

```
/data/cities/          - one JSON file per city (see schema below)
/data/collections/     - one JSON file per collection page (hand-curated, Contract D)
/data/species/         - one JSON file per species page intro (hand-written, gates Contract F)
/data/countries/       - one JSON file per country page intro (hand-written, gates Contract G)
/data/city-list.json   - every city and its status (the prioritised ORDER lives in CITY_QUEUE.md)
/site/                 - static site generator (Astro or similar)
/scripts/              - automation scripts
/CURATION.md           - log of what could not be verified, and notes worth keeping. Not a to-do list for Hidde.
/SEO_GEO_BLUEPRINT.md  - page contracts (titles, schema, internal links, content minima). No page ships without conforming to it.
/TONE_OF_VOICE.md      - the voice for all stories and page copy. Paris is the calibration standard.
/LOG.md                - what each autonomous run did. Hidde's catch-up file.
```

```json
    }
  ]

```

## The research workflow (autonomous run)

**The capacity doctrine, ruled by Hidde 2026-07-28, replacing night-runs-only:** the goal is optimal use of his subscription, not politeness by clock. Runs fire day and night, and every check fails OPEN into working, because his rule is that silence means the machine runs.

**How they actually fire, corrected 2026-08-12 because this paragraph claimed something untrue for twelve days.** It said runs chain themselves around the clock, and they do not: self-chaining was switched off on 2026-07-31 (commit c034207) when a chaining day spent ~940 minutes and ate most of the week's usage in two days. The only trigger is nightly.yml's cron knocks, twelve a day since 2026-08-28 (every two hours round the clock, on Hidde's "i think we can increase the amount of nightruns... since tokens are ok"; nine before that, which left 10:00 to 18:00 UTC empty), each capped at 120 minutes since 2026-08-17/19 (was 60 before that), which is what DATA.md's utilization meter has been counting all along. **That sentence used to read "the ceiling has never been the constraint and the meter proves it", and it was measuring the wrong ceiling. Corrected 2026-08-29.** The DAILY ceiling is indeed never binding: the best day spent 329 minutes of a possible 1080. The WEEKLY one is binding and always has been. The rolling seven-day total of machine minutes crossed 1,000 on 08-23 (1,170) and every knock for the next two and a half days died in seconds on the usage limit: 08-25 got through zero minutes. It fell back under 1,000 on 08-26 and the machine came back the same morning. So the budget is roughly **1,000 machine minutes in any seven days**, shared with Hidde's own sessions, and `python3 scripts/run_health.py --week` prints where the week stands. Read every schedule decision through that number: knocks are free, windows are not, and one good night can buy three dead days.

   What limits output inside that budget is RUN LENGTH, not knock count, and the difference is not marginal. Measured over the 66 working runs since 2026-08-15: runs under 20 minutes spent 324 minutes and produced 13 trees, one every 25 minutes; runs of 40 to 70 minutes produced one every 2.4 minutes, ten times better. A third of the machine's week was going into short runs that orient, claim and go home. Every window is 120 minutes and the average run handed 93 of them back.

   **The cause was the continuation's own probe, and it asked the wrong question.** It continued a window only when the attempt had shipped NOTHING, so a run that shipped eight trees in 28 minutes was told it was fine and the window closed with 92 minutes unspent. Productivity was punished with an early night. Since 2026-08-29 the probe asks how much of the WINDOW is left, a window gets up to four attempts, and it stops for the four reasons that are real: an error, a usage-limit death (under five turns), fewer than 25 minutes remaining, or the week's budget being spent. **And the brake is the limit itself, felt rather than predicted (Hidde, 2026-08-29: "ik verbruik zelf minder tokens dus is er meer over voor runs").** He is right, and the 1,000 was a guess dressed as a measurement: it came from the one week the machine hit the wall, and how much room exists depends on how much of the subscription he is using himself, which changes week to week. No number written here can know that. The limit CAN be felt, because an attempt it kills leaves an exact fingerprint in run-health.json: a result record saying turns 1, minutes 0.0, subtype success, the agent having started, asked and been refused. A run killed any other way (the bot-actor refusal of 08-28, a cancelled job) has no result record at all and null turns, so the two never get confused. `recent_limit_deaths()` counts the first kind, and two in six hours stops a window extending, because extending into a shut window buys nothing. When he is quiet there are none and the machine runs long; when he is busy it yields within hours without anybody touching a setting. Checked against the blackout: the brake is ON through 08-25 and 08-26 and OFF on 08-23, the best night the machine has had. The two budgets behind it are runaway backstops rather than predictions, `WEEK_BUDGET_MINUTES` 1400 and `DAY_BUDGET_MINUTES` 200, and they stay Hidde's numbers because it is his usage window. **And a knock that never ARRIVES is a third failure mode, invisible to run-health.json because that file only records runs that started**: GitHub delivered 2 of 9 on both 08-27 and 08-28. `health.py` counts arrivals in 24 hours and the digest's watchdog dispatches one when too few came, which is what it should have been doing instead of waiting for 26 hours of total silence.

   **And the delivery is now dispatched rather than hoped for, 2026-08-29, because the cron went from unreliable to mostly absent.** Since the twelve-knock line went live at 11:45 UTC on 08-28, eleven scheduled knocks were due and TWO arrived; the digest's watchdog could not close that, because it runs once a day and can therefore rescue at most one knock in twenty-four hours. The cause is documented GitHub behaviour and not a bug here: a scheduled run delayed under load is dropped once the delay reaches the next slot, and the two arrivals were 1h44 and 34 minutes late, so the delay is already the size of the spacing. `.github/workflows/knock.yml` checks every twenty minutes and dispatches a night run when nothing has started for two hours, which a workflow_dispatch never gets dropped for. It raises no ceiling: it holds the same twelve a day, two hours apart, never stacked on a run in flight. It is a watchdog and NOT the chain, which stays off: a chain re-fires when a run ends, this fires only into a two-hour silence. Worse, the disabling was itself broken: the presence-beacon check and a 10-to-30-minute sleep still ran before an `exit 0` that sat one line above the dispatch it was meant to suppress, so every run ended by sleeping for nothing and the presence beacon it consulted could not affect anything. That step is now four honest lines. The SessionStart presence beacon still fires and is still the right mechanism, but nothing reads it while the chain is off; turning the chain back on is Hidde's call, since it is his usage window, and the version to restore is this file as it stood before c034207.

Attempts that hit the usage limit die in seconds and cost nothing. Commit the smallest complete step first, always: a cutoff must cost minutes, not items. DATA.md's daily entry carries the utilization meter (attempts versus got-through) so nobody has to guess whether the limits are being used.

Each run, do exactly this, in order:

### Step 0: Read state and pick the work

Read `/data/city-queue.json` (the source file: every city's rank, score, tree target and measured state) and the published city files, then take the first item on this ladder that applies. Do one thing per run and do it properly; a half-researched city is worse than none.

1. **Unprocessed submissions** (see Step 0b). Someone cared enough to send something, that outranks everything.
2. **The site is broken. Ask with one command: `python3 scripts/health.py`.** It answers the whole rung, exit 0 for clear and 1 for something to deal with: the newest Smoke test and deploy conclusions, whether the Data digest or Fresh-eyes review has gone past 26 hours or the Weekly analysis past 8 days (GitHub drops schedules silently, and the digest's own watchdog cannot report a digest that never ran), and whether REVIEW.md's newest entry holds a BLOCKER. It prints the `gh workflow run` line to fix a stale one. Written 2026-08-17 because doing this by hand is four `gh` calls plus three thresholds held in your head, and a check that costs that much gets skipped on a short window.

   A BLOCKER outranks all new coverage, and it is answered rather than obeyed: read the finding, then check its evidence before acting on it, because the reviewer can only see what we published and a finding built on our own invented sentence inherits the invention. The worked example is the same day this rung was scripted: a BLOCKER said Milan's Archdevil stood in a courtyard of a dwelling, and the courtyard was a bridge claim in our own story. The comune's own page says park, and grants gate access. Retiring the tree would have obeyed the BLOCKER and lost a good entry.

   When a BLOCKER is dealt with, record it: `python3 scripts/health.py --answer <review-date> "<what was done>"`. REVIEW.md is append-only and the next review is a day away, so without that ledger every knock in between re-serves the same finding and a run re-litigates what is already fixed.

   Also at this rung, and it needs a session rather than a run: a finding that turns on a host in `data/fetch-blocklist.json` cannot be checked by a night run at all. Say so and leave it; do not retire a tree to clear a BLOCKER you could not verify.
3. **Something published is wrong**, as opposed to merely imprecise: a tree that has fallen, a pin in the wrong place, a fact that does not hold up. Wrong costs trust; vague does not, as long as it says so.
4. **New coverage: work top-down through `CITY_QUEUE.md`.** That file is the single city list, the single order and the definition of how far to take a city, written 2026-08-11 after Hidde pointed out that six prioritisations were running at once ("there are multiple city prioritisations living amongst each other"). It replaces the wave order, the 2026-08-06 tourist rollout and its phase-1 list, the 2026-08-06 working order, the 2026-08-09 Porto-and-Lisbon ruling (absorbed: they score 1 and 2), and the order embedded in nightly.yml. Do not re-derive a queue here or anywhere else; if this file and CITY_QUEUE.md ever disagree, that one wins and this paragraph is the stale copy.

   The rule it applies, in one line, because a run should understand it rather than obey it blindly: score is demand times our own realised yield, and yield falls sharply with fame, so a mid-sized city where nobody else writes about trees beats a famous one where everybody does. A published page that has never once appeared in a digest's top pages counts as measured evidence of near-zero yield, not as missing data.

   What did NOT change: the four-tree floor, the London gate (no Woodland Trust data without written permission), and the rule that only the hard rules and an unstatable location stop a tree going live.

   **Scout the register BEFORE researching a city from zero, and let the queue pick which register (Hidde, 2026-08-16: "i want to make use of all usefull registers if it speeds up the data building process").** Run `python3 scripts/scout_next.py --target`. It prints one line, either BUILD a named city or SCOUT a named country, and the rule behind it is: take the highest-ranked city still below target, and if it has no supply and no recorded verdict, scout the register behind THAT country first. A register cuts a city from ~19k tokens per tree to ~0.4k, so an hour spent finding one is an hour that makes every later hour cheaper.

   Two boundaries, both learned the day this was written. **Scouting is targeted, never a nightly sweep**: the ArcGIS and EU sweeps below return the same answers most nights, and re-running an exhausted hunt is this project's most repeated waste. And **the queue picks the target, not the data**: a session drifted into Austrian Naturdenkmal registers serving Innsbruck (#194) and Hallstatt (#219) while Sintra (#32) and Bari (#41) had never been looked at, because it was choosing by how many trees a country held rather than by rank. Hidde caught it: "I dont want random austrian cities i need new registers that help with the 250 page."

   **Out of focus, ruled by Hidde 2026-08-16: Israel, Africa, India and Russia.** His words: "israel africa india and russia ar out of focus". No scouting window is spent on them and `scout_next.py` skips them, so `--target` never points there. This is a SCOPE decision and not a judgement about the trees: the queue still ranks those cities, nothing published is removed, and a reader submission from one of them is processed like any other. It affects three ranked cities as of that date (Jerusalem #51, Tel Aviv #143, Cape Town #171) and closes the half-finished Israel scout rather than leaving it stalled. The list lives in `data/register-scouting.json` under `out_of_focus`, and reversing it means deleting a line there.

   **When every city at the top has either supply or a written verdict, there is nothing to scout and the answer is build.** That is not hypothetical: of the top 60 cities below target on 2026-08-16, 24 had no supply, and only 14 of those had never been scouted. The other 10 needed permission or a non-register source, and scouting them again would have bought nothing. `data/register-scouting.json` is where those verdicts live, one line each, so the check is a lookup rather than a rediscovery.

5. **Register-layer countries, licence-led.** The parallel quantity track (see "The register layer" below): scout, import and map official registers, in the order the data itself allows; a run picks whichever of rung 4 and 5 it can move furthest in its window, trip cities always winning ties.
6. **Photos and depth on published cities.** The 8-of-10 photo target and pin work continue as the background lane, no longer gating anything (the 13 documented dead-end gaps from the depth phase wait for UGC or new sources; do not re-run exhausted hunts). Hunt photos for NEW cities during their research, hard, per Step 4; two honesty rules never bend: no photo without a verified open licence and attribution, and a genuinely unreachable target is recorded in CURATION.md, never looped on.

   **One photograph per city is a standing aim, and it ranks BELOW five trees (Hidde, 2026-08-17: "can we make part of the nightly runs that we aim to get at least 1 photo per city", then "focus should be first get it to +5 trees than get photos").** A large minority of published cities have no photograph at all (`python3 scripts/stats.py` prints today's count; it was 45 of 127 on 2026-08-17 and the shape of the problem has not changed). The order he set is not a detail: a city under five trees has a TREE problem, and a photograph on a three-tree page does not make it an afternoon out. So a city joins the photo aim only once it clears five, and `scripts/photo_gaps.py` enforces that split rather than leaving it to memory (`--shortlist` prints one candidate per photo-less city, biggest city first).

   **The split between the two kinds of run is GONE, and it was never physical (measured 2026-09-01).** This paragraph said a night run cannot judge a photograph because the CI runner's egress proxy blocks upload.wikimedia.org, and that was wrong for 25 days. Probed from the runner: the full-size file returns 200 and 7.4 MB of JPEG, the 500px and 960px thumbnails return 200, the Commons API, Openverse and iNaturalist all return 200. What actually happened on 2026-08-07 was a 400 on a thumbnail width Wikimedia does not render (it serves fixed buckets, 250/330/500/960, which `site/src/lib/images.ts` had already probed and written down), and a whole window went into reading that as a network block. It is the same failure this file records about the Dutch register licence: a verdict that closed a door outlived the fact, and nobody re-opened it. **So a night run may take a viewing pass**, and the machinery for it already exists end to end: `photo_fetch.py --zero --out <dir> --per-tree 3` downloads the candidates serialised at one every three seconds and scores each one's exposure, the run READS the files it wrote, and `photo_verdicts.py` applies the verdicts in one serialised sweep. What does not move an inch is the honesty half, which was always the real rule: look at the actual pixels before approving, and where two similar trees stand nearby the status is `held`, not `approved`. This matters most exactly when Hidde is away: 3,395 candidates sit queued against 1,367 trees, and until now every one of them waited on his own Mac.

   **What a viewing pass learned on 2026-08-16, so the next one does not relearn it.** Four candidates were judged and three were rejected, and only one of the three failed on picture quality. Copenhagen's pacifier tree was a good photograph under a free licence in the right city of the right species, and it was **the wrong tree**, three kilometres away in another park, because Denmark has several. Cagliari's file had been attached by the sweep to two different trees at once. So the filename matching in `photo_gaps.py` decides what is worth LOOKING at and never what ships; a geotag settles which trunk it is when the file carries one, and where it does not and two similar trees stand nearby, the honest status is `held`, not `approved`.

   **And the cheaper route, when a city's own trees are simply unphotographed:** `famous_trees.py` walks Commons' named-tree categories and found **929 famous trees we do not map, 926 of which arrive with a photograph already attached.** Adding a tree that brings its own picture beats hunting a picture for a tree nobody has photographed, and it adds a tree people have heard of. Two of Denmark's sit 12 and 13 km from Copenhagen, inside the day-trip boundary. Treat that file as a research list, never an import: its name matching is deliberately loose and has produced nonsense (Hobart's dawn redwoods matched a 1920 street scene in Bundaberg).
7. **Page gaps: the species, country and park pages the data has already earned.** `python3 scripts/pagegaps.py` names them, biggest first. A species with a dozen trees and no page, or a country with fifty and no page, is an entry from Google that does not exist while the trees behind it do, and writing the intro is small, cheap work that belongs in the ordinary batch rather than in a project of its own. It sits here rather than higher because a page grouping trees is worth less than the trees themselves; it sits above product work because it is content and it compounds with every city opened. Hidde, 2026-08-13, on the gate that had held these back: "wat mij betreft mogen die intros gewoon geautomatiseerd geschreven worden, ik snap niet waar we zo moeilijk over doen, bouw gewoon." Nothing in SEO_GEO_BLUEPRINT.md changes: Contracts F and G require that a page HAS a written intro, never that a human typed it. What does not bend is P3, so each intro is written from that subject's own trees and never from a pattern.

8. **Product work, when everything above is satisfied.** Draw the top unblocked item from `PRODUCT_TODO.md` and finish it completely. That lane exists because runs used to be allowed only to research trees while the product itself waited for a human session; Hidde closed that gap on 2026-07-26. Its rules live in the file and are strict, because a CI run cannot see the page it changes: reversible only, contracts must validate, every item verified by build output or grep rather than eyes, no visual-taste work and nothing from the hard list.

**Report a digest in TABLES, never in prose (Hidde, 2026-08-12: "kun je je digest vanaf nu altijd in tabellen geven dit is onleesbaar").** DATA.md is already written as tables; re-narrating those numbers into paragraphs is how a readable file becomes an unreadable summary. So a session reporting the daily or weekly numbers gives: one table for the search window, one for the pages that win and lose, one for the product funnel, **one for the people who signed up**, **one for what people typed into our own search**, **one for what happened on the site itself** (Cloudflare's beacon: visits, pageviews, the pages they looked at, where they came from), **one for what the night shift did**, **one for what people did in the APP**, and at most two sentences of reading underneath.

**The site table is on the list because it was in DATA.md and kept falling out of the report (Hidde, 2026-08-22: "wat ik mis in de daily digest is het aantal gebruikers op de site en de acties en de hoeveelheid paginas en hoe ze navigeren").** Search Console says who could have come; the beacon says who did, on what pages, from where, on which device. Two limits belong beside it every time, because without them the numbers get over-read: Cloudflare buckets counts to the nearest ten, so a daily figure of 40 is anywhere from 35 to 44, and the beacon is cookieless, so there is no session and page-to-page navigation cannot be measured at all. What stands in for navigation is the share of pageviews whose referrer is our own host, plus pages per visit.

**The search-terms table is on the list because the data was being written and never read (Hidde, 2026-08-29: "meten we eigenlijk wat mensen intoetsen op zoeken").** We have measured it since the search box was built: `search-form.ts` sends the typed text as the event's `detail` and it has been sitting in the events table ever since, while the digest selected `name` and never `detail`. It is worth more per row than anything else on the page, because Search Console says what somebody typed into GOOGLE and never arrived from, while this says what somebody standing on our own site went looking for. **A term that returns nothing is a request**, and it is the only line on the page that names a tree or a place we do not have.

**And the machine's table covers the LAST 24 HOURS from the moment it is read, not "the night" (Hidde, 2026-08-29: "kun je niet de nachturns doen van de nacht maar van de afgelopen 24uur vanaf nu altijd bij digest").** The old window started at 18:00 the previous day, which was right while the machine only worked at night. Since the twelve-knock line went live on 2026-08-28 it runs every two hours round the clock, so that window dropped everything between this morning and now, which is exactly the part he has not seen. Measured the day it changed: 8 runs in the rolling day against 2 in the old window.

**The night-shift table is part of every digest report and gets passed through, never dropped (Hidde, 2026-08-21: "how can i make you remember to add the results of the nightruns").** DATA.md has carried "What the night shift did" since 2026-08-18, per run: minutes, trees, commits, refusals, **which cities it touched and how many trees each gained**, plus the machine's own commit subjects. The city column is his (2026-08-24: "kun je ook altijd vertellen welke steden de nachtruns hebben gedaan"), and it replaced the turns column, which was a number nobody ever acted on. It is written by run_health.py rather than assembled by a session, so a digest cannot report the night without naming the cities. On 2026-08-21 a session reported the digest from this file's table list, which did not name it, so it silently fell out. The fix is this sentence: the list above is the contract, and the night-shift table is on it.

**The app table is its own table, ruled by Hidde 2026-08-30 ("eigen tabel"), and it is empty until the app has users.** The app measured nothing at all until that day; it now sends seven events to our own PostHog project on their EU cloud (`Kit/Measure.swift`, and CONVENTIONS.md for why PostHog rather than the SDK everybody else ships). The read key sits in the `POSTHOG_READ_KEY` secret, so a digest can query it without anybody pasting anything.

It gets its own table for the same reason the signups do: site and app are different people at different moments, and folded together neither one has a shape. The website's beacon says what somebody did on a page; this says what somebody did while standing outside holding the thing. Opens, the tabs they moved to, trees opened, Take me there, saves, ticks, and trees they added themselves.

The honesty rule the whole file runs on applies harder here than anywhere: for a long while this table is one person, and a single tap is never a trend.

**The search-window table is DAY BY DAY, the last 7 days, one row each (Hidde, 2026-08-17).** Clicks, impressions, CTR and average position per day, with a totals row underneath. Not a window total against the window before it, and not a hand-picked best day: those were the summary a session reached for on 2026-08-17, and they hide the only thing the table is for, which is seeing the shape of the last week at a glance. DATA.md already writes the daily rows; the job is to pass them through, trimmed to seven, never to average them into a verdict. The sentences carry the judgement, the tables carry the numbers, and the numbers never get buried in a paragraph.

**The signup table is not optional and gets its own table (Hidde, 2026-08-16: "waarom heb je trouwens niks over de app inschrijvingen gezegd in de daily digest die moet daar in onthou dat en account registraties").** It carries **app waitlist signups, account registrations, saves, and reader submissions split in two**, each with yesterday's number, the 14-day number and the running total. A digest that folds them into one line of a funnel table has technically reported them and has actually hidden them, which is what happened on 2026-08-16. Saves joined on Hidde's ask (2026-08-21): a save is a person keeping a tree, the closest thing to collecting the site has. The submissions split is his too, same day ("a reader submission adding a tree is very different from a thumbs up"): **a tree or city sent through the contribute form (kind `tree`/`city`) is supply and a contributor; a worth-it report or correction (kind `feedback`/`correction`/`privacy`) is a reaction.** Both are people, only one is a tree we did not have, and the digest and any session reading it name which kind arrived, never the flattering generic "a submission".

They get their own table because they are the only numbers on the page that are PEOPLE rather than traffic, and phase 1 is explicitly about collecting people and data (see "The two phases"). Impressions measure whether Google can see us; a waitlist signup measures whether a human wants the thing. At these volumes the second is worth more per unit than the first, and a single-digit number in a shared row reads as noise when it is the most important number of the day. The search window table can be flat for a week and mean nothing; one account registration means somebody typed their email address into a site they had never heard of that morning.

   **(0b) TRANSLATE A PAGE THAT ALREADY EARNS IMPRESSIONS (Hidde, 2026-09-01, on being shown the ten-day readback: "ja graag").** Cheapest supply on this ladder per impression, because it produces no new facts at all: the trees, the pins, the photographs, the licences and the stories already exist, and a translation is an overlay on the canonical city file (Contract J). Measured over the first ten days of the seven-language rollout, 23 translated sets carried **6.8 percent of the site's impressions and 7.2 percent of its clicks** (683 of 10,027 and 16 of 223), at a CTR of 2.3 against the site's 2.2, and still climbing every day of the window. Five sets had already passed their English twin.

That is also its precondition, and it is the whole discipline of this rung: **translate a page whose ENGLISH twin already takes impressions.** Translating a page nobody finds in English gives a page nobody finds in two languages.

And it fills LEADS, never `data/cities`: a lead becoming writable is not a tree going live, and a write pass still looks at every one.

### Step 1: Research the city's trees (BE THOROUGH)

**The assembly line, ruled by Hidde 2026-08-06: tokens per tree must come down.** The 2026-08-05 retro measured 32,870 tokens per published tree against 19,000 the week before, and the diagnosis is structural: each pass carried a fixed cost of 150k to 200k regardless of yield, because one agent did five jobs (search, verify, write, hunt photos, format) and read 86KB of corpus before starting. Five changes, all binding:

1. **Every brief is generated, never written from memory: `python3 scripts/passcheck.py --brief <place>`.** It prints the paste-ready brief: live status, ids, the actual register candidates with coordinates, what earlier passes found or rejected, the hosts that hang. Three briefs left on a false premise on 2026-08-05 (Napoli, Firenze, Munich); a generated brief cannot, because it is built from the data the premise would contradict. The proof case came the same day the flag was written: the 2026-08-06 handover listed Valencia as "never done" while it was live with 10 trees.

   **And every pass is claimed before it is dispatched: `python3 scripts/passcheck.py --claim <place> --kind verify|write|photo`, then commit and push the claim, then dispatch.** Release with `--release <place>` when the output is merged. This exists because passcheck knew what was published and nothing knew what was being worked on: on 2026-08-06 a session write pass and a night run wrote the same nine Padova stories twenty minutes apart, 89k tokens for nothing. The claim lives in data/in-flight.json, expires after 4 hours on its own (a dead session never blocks a city), matches across name spellings (a claim on Padova blocks Padua), and passcheck refuses to print a brief for a claimed place. It is a courtesy lock, not a mutex: it is only visible once pushed, so the push is part of the claim, and it catches the minutes-apart collisions that actually happen here, not seconds-apart races.
2. **A pass is one of two kinds, never both.** A **verification pass** confirms alive, sources, species, age and pin, delivers structured facts with `verify_notes`, writes zero prose and hunts zero photos; its whole rulebook is `BRIEF_RESEARCH.md` and it does not read CLAUDE.md. A **writing pass** turns 15 to 20 verified trees ACROSS cities into stories in one context per `BRIEF_WRITING.md`, so the tone calibration is read once, not per city. The main session merges, rebuilds, runs QA and commits.
3. **Model tiering, mechanical since 2026-08-06 (Hidde: the line itself must pick the right AI).** The tiering lives in the agent definitions under `.claude/agents/`, not in anyone's memory: `verify` pins Sonnet (retrieval-heavy; never the smallest model, because verification is where the fabrication risk lives), `write-stories` and `photo-judge` pin Opus (judgement about what ships). Dispatch a pass by its agent name and the model follows; the generated brief prints which agent to use. Dispatching outside these agents is what needs a reason. Anything deterministic (clustering, register parsing, photo candidate retrieval) is a script and costs nothing.
4. **No pass under six expected candidates.** The fixed cost makes small passes expensive by definition; thin targets wait in the queue until a batch (neighbouring cities, one register) carries the overhead.
5. **Photo hunting leaves research passes entirely**, superseding the three-photos-per-pass rule of 2026-08-05: `scripts/photo_hunt.py` sweeps Commons by API into `data/photo-queue.json` (measured agent cost was 72k tokens per usable photo; the API sweep costs none), and a periodic viewing pass judges queued candidates against the Cadiz standard, 20 to 30 images in one context. The two honesty rules are untouched: verified licence and attribution always, and look at the pixels before approving.

The target on the meter: **at or under 15,000 tokens per published tree**, read weekly from RETRO.md. Every pass still logs to `data/agent-costs.json` (kinds now include `verify` and `write`); if the line does not come down, the werkwijze moves again, and that is a session decision with Hidde, not a run's.

**The current course, approved by Hidde 2026-08-03: register first, cluster over count, batch by country.** It replaces city-by-city web research for the three focus countries, because all three have an official register that gives species, age and often coordinates in one document.

- **Import the register once, then research becomes selection.** Spain: the Andalusian catalogue plus the municipal catalogues (Cordoba's held 54 trees with GPS, girth and dating in one PDF). Portugal: ICNF, already imported. Japan: the Environment Ministry's giant-tree database, which records every tree in the country over 300 cm girth with measurements, plus the national and prefectural Natural Monument designations. With the register in hand a city is "pick the best from an already-verified list and write", not "search the web", which is the difference between three hours and under one.
- **Choose walkable clusters, not famous singletons.** Measured 2026-08-03 across all 81 published cities: Cadiz spans 600 metres with all five trees inside two kilometres, York 100 metres, Rotterdam 900. Kyoto spans 35 km with one tree within two kilometres of any other, Hong Kong 45 km. Twenty cities have fewer than half their trees within walking distance of each other. That, not the count, is why Cadiz reads as a good afternoon and Kyoto does not. So: research a district at a time rather than a metropolis, and prefer a tree that joins a cluster over a marginally better one that stands alone. A large city gets several clusters of five to eight; a small one gets a single cluster of four to six. Density wins ties (the ten-per-city default it used to cap this with was replaced on 2026-08-12 by CITY_QUEUE.md's computed target).
- **Batch by country, not by city, in two passes (Hidde, 2026-08-03: "is de snelste route niet een run voor een heel land te doen, en dan per stad stuk voor stuk aanvullen").** The country pass is cheap because most of it is arithmetic rather than research: import the register, then run `python3 scripts/cluster_register.py data/registers/[file].json` to group every entry into what can actually be walked between. On Portugal's 124 registered trees that produces 9 walkable clusters covering 106 of them, with Lisbon falling out as three separate walks rather than one list. The city pass then starts from "verify these six trees around this point", which is a far smaller job than "find the ten best trees in this metropolis". What the script cannot do, and a run still must: decide whether a designated tree is actually remarkable, find the second source, and write it. A register says protected, not worth the walk.
- **How many trees a city gets, now that clusters decide it.** The floor is four verified trees or no page, with the single-famous-tree exception of 2026-08-31 above. A walk is four to eight trees inside roughly two kilometres, because past eight it stops being an afternoon. A compact city is one cluster and is finished at five or six, which is why Cadiz ships five and reads better than cities with ten. A large city is several clusters, and the tenth tree should complete a second walk rather than add a lonely eleventh location on the far side of town. The count is an output of the clustering rather than a target to fill; the ceiling is CITY_QUEUE.md's computed target, not the old ten.
- **The regional register before the national one, where both exist (learned across Caserta and Rome, 2026-08-05).** Italy's national MASAF file has no age field and no vitality field, which are the two things a page needs most. The regions fill both. Campania publishes a per-tree card at `agricoltura.regione.campania.it/foreste/monum/scheda_NNN.html` with an age estimate and a description; one scrape gave the second source for nearly every tree in Caserta. Lazio publishes an xlsx carrying the reason each tree was designated, a notes column saying which girth figures are stem lists rather than trunks, and **a second sheet of removed trees with reasons**, on which five Roma trees sit as dead while still live in the national file. So: find the regional list first, and treat the national register as the index rather than the source.
- **Three photos per city per pass, maximum (Hidde, 2026-08-05).** Photo hunting is the least productive hour in a research pass and it expands to fill whatever time it is given. Three good ones make the page; the rest is an honest gap that a later pass or a reader can close.
- **Photo gaps close faster.** Search Commons by name, by category and by geosearch for the shortlist only, then stop. Measured yield across three cities researched 2026-08-03: 2 usable photos of 12 candidates in Cordoba, 4 of 10 in Nara, 6 of 11 in Fukuoka. It is the least productive hour in a research pass.

**Search in this order (Hidde, 2026-08-02: the point is faster runs per city, not more searching).** Each step is narrower and higher-yield than the generic sweep, so most cities are done before the last step. Stop when the city has its trees; do not run every step for its own sake.

1. **Name the places first, then search the places.** Spend the first five minutes listing the city's historic parks, botanical gardens, palace and estate grounds, and its churchyards, cathedral closes, monasteries, convent gardens, temple and shrine precincts and old cemeteries. Wikipedia's city page, the municipal parks department and a tourist map give this list in minutes. Then search each named site plus a species word, in English and the local language (kerk, klooster, iglesia, ermita, chiesa, igreja, Kirche, jinja, taisha, -ji). This finds trees no listicle mentions, because the ground was protected and someone tended it for centuries; on sacred ground the tree often predates the building beside it. Evidence: Lisbon's Jardim da Estrela (giant ficus, jacarandas) was missed entirely by the city-level sweep. **The "26 percent of our trees stand on sacred ground" figure that stood here was withdrawn on 2026-08-08: it does not survive checking.** It was produced by keyword-matching, which counts street names (Plantage Kerklaan is the address of Amsterdam's zoo), tree nicknames (the Lomanstraat "Cathedral of Trees") and Portuguese administrative parishes; under strict matching it falls below 8 percent and most of that is still false positives. The search technique is still right and still finds trees a listicle misses. The statistic was not, and it is recorded here rather than quietly deleted because a run quoted it as fact.
2. **Ask whether the city has already published its own tree route (learned from Setubal, 2026-08-04).** Search for "[city] roteiro das arvores", "[stad] bomenroute", "[ciudad] ruta de arboles singulares", "[city] tree trail", plus the municipality's own environment or parks pages. Setubal's parish publishes a tree-by-tree walking route with species, place, age and height, which independently corroborated nine of our ten entries and solved the single most expensive part of a research pass, the second source, in one fetch. Many European municipalities do this and almost nobody links to it. Check it before any generic searching.
3. **The local newspaper's own survey of the city's monumental trees (learned from Trieste, 2026-08-05).** Search "[citta] alberi monumentali" plus the local title, "[stad] monumentale bomen" plus the regional paper, and the same in any language. A paper that has written a piece on "the city's oldest trees" names individual trees, gives ages, says which is oldest and reports what the council has done about them. Il Piccolo's survey of Trieste's 48 monumental trees supplied in one fetch the ages that neither the national nor the regional register carries, because no register anywhere has an age field for Italy. It is a source to verify against, never to copy from, and it usually beats step 4 outright.
4. **The official register, if the country has one.** A municipal or national list of protected/monumental trees is the fastest verification there is: it gives species, age and often coordinates in one document. OPEN_DATA_SURVEY.md records which countries have usable ones and under which licence.
5. **The generic sweep**, for whatever the first two missed: "[city] oldest trees", "[city] remarkable trees", "[city] monumental trees", "[city] famous historic trees", in English AND the local language.
6. **Cross-check and fill:** monumentaltrees.com for verification of age and location claims only (NEVER copy their photos or text), Atlas Obscura, local history blogs, park authority pages.

- A tree qualifies if it is: genuinely old OR visually spectacular OR historically significant, AND publicly accessible
- **The day-trip boundary (Hidde, 2026-08-01):** a tree within roughly 30 minutes by public transport of the city centre belongs on the city page, labeled honestly: the real place name in its location fields, the true travel time in transport. Blarney counts for Cork, Fota counts for Cork, Coole Park counts for Galway. What it does not allow: quietly presenting an out-of-town tree as standing in the city.

**The count follows the trees. Decided by Hidde 2026-07-26, reframed by him 2026-08-02, and given a number per city on 2026-08-12.** Ten was always the working default rather than a law: "de enige reden waarom ik met 10 begin is SEO strategie en haalbaarheid... uiteindelijk is de visie dat er een behapbaar aantal bomen in de buurt te vinden zijn, geen exacte nummers".

**The staircase (Hidde, 2026-08-13), and it is the whole phase-1 plan: sprint every ranked city in the top 250 to 10 trees first, then deepen only what Google confirms, by city size.** Deepening is paused while the sprint runs ("voorrang in deze top 250 tot de 10 brengen en niet verdiepen", same day): stage 2 is dispatched only when stage 1 has nothing left that moves cheaply. Stadje 10, grote stad 20, mega stad 30, metropool max 50, with demand (Wikipedia pageviews) as the size proxy and "confirmed" meaning Search Console shows the city impressions. His 80/20 rule governs reaching any number: the moment the next tree gets hard to find, the city is done for now and the run moves on ("eeuwig tokens gebruiken tot deze max te halen is niet de strategie"). The full rule, its thresholds and its superseded versions live in `CITY_QUEUE.md` and nowhere else; `data/city-queue.json` carries the number per city and re-ranks itself daily from the digest's Search Console readback, so priority updates come from the data and never wait on Hidde. `python3 scripts/city_queue.py --next` prints both stages in order. A target is a ceiling and a stopping point, never a quota and never a floor: a city that runs out of trees clearing the bar is finished below it (Cadiz at 5), and a city above it (Barcelona at 46) is finished, with nothing ever removed to meet a number.

A city may grow past its target when genuinely remarkable trees turn up later or a reader sends one in. Growing a city is normal work, not an exception to argue for. What the reframing does NOT license: padding. The bar per tree is unchanged, and a city still ships the trees that clear it, never a number filled to look finished. When a city passes ten, its page title and question page must stop saying ten. The doctrine bends the number in both directions, and only ever to follow the trees.

**Being ninth in line is not a reason to hold a tree back (Hidde, 2026-08-10, on finding 43 leads blocked on the count: "this one also doesn't make sense").** This paragraph used to end by contradicting itself, saying in one breath that a city may grow past ten and in the next that "a city with five hundred worthy candidates still ships exactly ten" and that the number "bends downward only, never up". Those were leftovers from before the 2026-08-02 reframe, and they were not harmless: a run looking for permission to refuse found it, 43 times. They are deleted. What made collecting work was never the number, it was the bar per tree, and the bar is untouched.

A capital with ten genuinely remarkable trees gets ten; a smaller city gets the five or eight that truly clear the bar; padding a list to reach ten is fabrication's polite cousin and forbidden (Verona shipping 8 was right). A place earns a page only when at least four trees survive verification; below that it gets no page yet. Exclusivity is the point: a collector wants every entry to deserve its spot, and a two-street village with one fine oak is a collection entry, not a city.

**One exception, ruled by Hidde 2026-08-31: a place may publish below four when a single tree is itself the destination.** The test is one question and it is deliberately hard to abuse: would somebody travel specifically for THIS ONE TREE? The Angel Oak yes, the Castagno dei Cento Cavalli yes, a fine oak in a village no. Everything else about the bar is untouched, and the exception is about fame rather than quality: a tree being good is not the test, a tree being the reason for the trip is.

**Why it exists, and it is not a loosening of the padding rule.** The floor was written to stop a list being padded to a number, and it still does that; 34 of 190 published places sit exactly at four. What it was also doing, unintentionally, was blocking the opposite case: a solitary world-famous tree with nothing else near it. That cost three real things in one day on 2026-08-31. The Castagno dei Cento Cavalli, roughly 2,200 years old, free, confirmed alive by its own comune in March 2026, had no home and a collection could not give it one, because collection entries point at trees that are already published. Bartram's Ginkgo, the oldest ginkgo in America, stood alone in Philadelphia. And the 60 famous American trees in `data/leads/_famous-united-states.json`, every one with a photograph, mostly stand by themselves.

**And the reason it is a floor exception rather than a new page type**, which was the alternative considered and rejected the same day: a tree published without a page is a worse tree. It appears on the map and nowhere else, so it has no story, no reason-it-is-special and no way to be collected, which kills three of the four verbs. Trees stay inside places; what changed is only how few trees a place may have. The corollary is that the CONTAINER should never be assumed to be a city (goal 3, 2026-08-14): Kauai, Menorca, Madeira, Crete and Sardinia are already places rather than cities, and Sardinia spans 128 km.

**An entry must be alive (Hidde, 2026-07-27: "we doen niet aan dode bomen").** The product is standing before something that has been alive for centuries; a carcass, stump or relic breaks that promise and makes the years-counter lie. A dead tree may appear inside a story as context, never as a collectible entry, however famous the remains (Queen Elizabeth's Oak in Greenwich and Vienna's Stock im Eisen both fail this test and are to be replaced; a fallen original honestly succeeded by a living replanting, like the Elm of Saint-Gervais, passes when the entry describes the living tree). **A tree we cannot re-confirm does not hold a city back (Hidde, 2026-08-06: "ik vind dat je heel spastisch bent over dode bomen. ja we willen die liever niet maar het hoeft niet een hele stad tegen te houden. een gebruiker mag ons ook ooit wijzen op deze is dood").** The rule above is about a dead tree as an ENTRY, not about a confidence threshold before a city may open. Zaragoza was held back because three of its four trees had no dated proof of life, and that was the rule applied harder than it says. Publish, and say what you know: where the source is old or the confirmation is thin, the page states it in the FAQ in plain words, names what has already been lost from that source, and asks the reader to tell us. Readers are the correction layer, which is the standing position everywhere else on this site. What does NOT change: a tree you know to be dead never ships, and a tree reported dead gets checked and removed the same day.

Runs: sweep CURATION.md's 2026-07-27 dead-tree suspect list, verify each against sources, and replace confirmed dead entries with living trees researched to the normal standard, one city per pass, with an honest note. Replacements reuse the old id and the page must redirect or the old URL stay resolvable, per the Barcelona bcn_008 precedent.

**At most about a third of a city may sit behind a ticket (Hidde, 2026-08-23).** Bomenstichting Amsterdam made the point twice and was right both times: ten of Amsterdam's thirty-nine trees stood behind paid entry at Artis or the Hortus, and "het blijft bezwaarlijk dat 10 bomen (een derde) niet gratis toegankelijk zijn." His ruling: "ik heb liever 34 goede bereikbare dan 39." Paid entry is still allowed and `access` still has to say so; what is not allowed is a city page that turns out to be a garden page, which is what happens when a run opens a city by taking the easiest eight trees out of one ticketed collection.

**The fix is almost never a deletion, and Leiden is the proof.** Five were pulled there the same day and three went straight back: the first zelkovas ever to leave Japan, the thickest horse chestnut in the Netherlands and a catalpa held out of the canal on cables are not collection filler, and cutting them to hit a ratio would have made the page worse. Only two were padding. So the test on each tree is the one this file already applies everywhere, would somebody cross town for it, and the answer to a bad ratio is FREE TREES ADDED rather than good ones removed. `check_paid_share()` in scripts/preflight.py prints the cities over the line as a NOTE rather than a FAIL for exactly that reason.

**When a tree does come off, nothing is thrown away and no URL breaks.** The story and sources go to `data/leads/[city].json` with the reason, and the slug goes into `REMOVED_TREE_SLUGS` in `site/src/lib/redirect-map.ts` so anything Google indexed keeps resolving (hard rule 3). Amsterdam's five and Leiden's two are the worked examples. And check the page copy afterwards: preflight caught Amsterdam's meta description, its question page and its access FAQ all still promising thirty-nine trees.

**An entry must be a collectible point (Hidde, 2026-07-26).** One identifiable tree, or an ensemble only when the ensemble itself is the destination: compact, famous for being exactly that, with one obvious place to stand. The Lomanstraat tunnel and the Meiji Jingu Gaien avenue pass; a thousand ordinary planes along a kilometre of La Rambla do not, there is nothing to stand at and nothing to have seen. Barcelona's bcn_008 failed this test as "The Plane Trees of La Rambla" and was replaced on 2026-07-26 with a genuinely singular tree, the Silk Tree of the Ciutadella (same id, so no URL broke). Apply the test to every future ensemble candidate.

**Work from the supply side, not from a quota (Hidde, 2026-08-04: "kunnen we niet beter op de bomen focussen die goedkoop tot onze beschikking liggen en die uitwerken ipv overal verplicht 10 bomen te maken").** He is right that the expensive tree is always the ninth in a thin city, while the first five are nearly free. One correction to his framing, and then it is exactly right: cheap and dense are different axes, and optimising for the cheapest TREE would give five fine trees in five different towns and a walk nowhere. **So rank by the cheapest CLUSTER, not the cheapest tree.**

The score is dense times data-rich: how many register trees sit within two kilometres of each other, multiplied by how many fields the register already fills per tree (species, name, age, girth, height), divided by the spread. A cluster of six inside 500 metres where the register gives species and age is the cheapest good city page that exists, because the state already made the selection and the research pass only has to verify and write.

**How to brief a research pass, and why the last one cost too much (2026-08-02/03).** Cordoba's research ran two and three quarter hours and came back with twelve candidates, an exhaustive list of rejects and a separate full photo sweep. Seven trees shipped. Nara's ran alongside it, was still going when the session closed, and was lost entirely, because a background agent lives in the session that started it. Both problems have the same shape: a brief that asks for everything, unbounded, with nothing written down until the end. So:

- **Write findings to disk as they are found, never only at the end.** A research pass appends each verified candidate to `data/research/[city].md` as it completes it. A pass that dies halfway then leaves half a city behind instead of nothing, and the next session continues rather than restarts.
- **Ask for what ships, not for everything.** Eight strong candidates is plenty for a ten-tree page; the ninth and tenth cost as much as the first eight and usually get cut.
- **Keep the rejects as data (Hidde, 2026-08-03: "we hebben ze uiteindelijk wss ooit nodig, ik verwacht dat het platform uiteindelijk alle remarkable trees van de wereld gaat mappen").** They go to `data/leads/[city].json`, never rendered, in two clearly separate kinds. **Leads** are trees that failed only on our evidence bar or on the count: undated, single-sourced, no photo, or simply ninth in line. Those are the raw material of the wider map, and a city that grows past ten starts here rather than from a blank search. **Blocked** are trees that must never ship whatever happens later: dead or gone, private land, an access-restricted site, a location its source withholds, or a non-collectible point like an avenue or a whole wood. Each blocked entry carries the reason, so a future pass reads why in one line instead of re-researching it and arriving at the same no. Cordoba's file is the worked example: eleven leads, seven blocked.

  **Widened by Hidde on 2026-08-05 into a standing rule: every tree a run comes across goes into the database, not only the ones a city pass rejected.** His words: "ik wil dat je alle bomen die je vangt in je onderzoek in je database opslaat vanaf nu, niet allemaal hoeven live te staan want sommige zullen misschien moeten worden geverifieerd, maar ik wil geen onderzoek meer voor niks zijn geweest." So a scouting sweep, a register scan, an Overpass query or a half-finished pass all land on disk before the session ends, in `data/leads/` when they are per-city candidates, in `data/registers/` when a register licence has been verified, and in `data/research/` when they are notes rather than trees. Nothing waits for the end of a pass: findings are appended as they are found, because two things have already been lost this way, a full Nara pass and a sweep agent whose four children finished after it had stopped. Publishing is a separate decision from keeping, and unverified is a fine reason not to publish and never a reason to throw away.
- **Photos are a second pass, on the shortlist only.** Hunting images for candidates that will not be published is the most expensive way to produce nothing.
- **Time-box it.** Say in the brief: report what you have after roughly forty minutes rather than continuing to completeness. An unbounded brief will always spend the whole window.
- **Prefer register-first countries when the window is short.** A national or municipal catalogue gives species, age and coordinates in one document; Portugal and Spain verify in minutes what takes an hour elsewhere.

**What registers get wrong, and what to check every time (learned on Portugal and Setubal, 2026-08-04).** A register is the cheapest source there is and it is not a clean one. Four checks, each of which has already caught a real error:

- **Units are not what the column says.** ICNF publishes trunk girth in metres; our import labelled the column girth_cm, so a 2.84 metre trunk read as under three centimetres and nobody noticed for days. Sanity-check every numeric field against the physical world before trusting it.
- **Fields go corrupt in ways that look plausible.** Three Setubal olives carry "2009" in the age column, a year sitting in a field that should hold a number of years. Publishing that unchecked would have given three ancient olives an age of nine.
- **Registers double-count what a visitor sees as one thing.** Three of Setubal's thirteen entries were twins: separately registered, metres apart, distinguishable only by girth. Shipping them separately would have produced near-identical pages. Fold twins into one entry that describes both, and record the folded ids as leads.
- **The register's total will not match the municipality's.** Setubal's council counts 21 classified trees against the 16 in the national register for the same municipality. Reconcile before any page quotes a national or city total, and never present a register count as a count of what exists.

Two more that follow from the same pass: an age in a register is the age at last measurement, so add the years since and say so; and no register anywhere carries a vitality field, so whether a tree still stands is always a per-tree check that the register cannot do for us.

**Where a week of tokens actually went, measured 2026-08-04.** The first full week of agent-driven work, totalled from the agents' own reported usage:

| Kind of work | Tokens | What it produced | Per unit |
|---|---|---|---|
| City research passes | ~620k | 32 published trees | 19k per tree |
| Photo hunting | ~145k | 2 usable photos | 72k per photo |
| Register scouting and import | ~460k | 1,048 known trees, plus a permanent map of what is usable per country | 0.4k per tree |
| Demand and SEO research | ~77k | the collection strategy, reusable | n/a |

Four conclusions, and they matter more than any single research technique:

1. **Scouting compounds; research does not.** Register work cost 460k once and now yields trees at near-zero forever, twenty-five times cheaper per tree than research and it keeps paying. Research costs its 19k per tree every single time. So an hour spent making the next hour cheaper beats an hour spent producing, and the instinct to "just do a city" is usually the expensive instinct.
2. **The waste was never in the work, it was in the coordination.** Roughly a fifth of the week bought nothing, and not one of those tokens was spent badly researching: a Nara pass died when the session closed because background agents live in the session that started them, a Fukuoka pass duplicated a night run already working the same city, and a photo hunt spent 145k to approve two images. All three are orchestration failures with cheap fixes, now recorded.
3. **Ask whether the task is judgement or retrieval before dispatching anything.** Retrieval is code and costs nothing: clustering a register, fetching a CSV, searching Commons by category. Judgement needs an agent: is this tree remarkable, does this source hold up, is this photo any good. The 72k-per-photo number is what it costs to make an agent do retrieval, and it is the single worst ratio the project has produced.
4. **A brief's cost is set by its stopping condition, not its length.** Adding "report what you have after roughly forty minutes" did more for Setubal's cost than every other instruction combined.

**Expensive work gets discussed before it starts (Hidde, 2026-08-04).** Before dispatching anything expected to run long or fan out wide, say in chat what it will cost and what it should return, and get his answer. This is his subscription and a research pass is not a free action: they run from 70k to 230k tokens each, and three in parallel is most of a working day's budget. Estimate, state it, then start. The exception is small bounded work whose cost is obviously trivial.

**Speed, as a standing instruction (Hidde, 2026-08-02: "sneller zou wel beter zijn").** A run's window is the scarce resource, so spend it on finding and verifying trees, not on re-deciding things that are already decided:

- **Check whether the city is already done or being done, before dispatching anything, and check it with `python3 scripts/backlog.py` rather than by eye.** `data/cities/[slug].json` existing means it shipped; the queue's status field and LOG.md's newest entries say what is in flight. On 2026-08-03 a night run published Fukuoka while a session research pass was already running on it, and the two overlapped on four trees out of ten. On 2026-08-05 two passes were sent out to "open" Napoli and Firenze while Naples and Florence had been live for weeks, because the queue was read by matching register place names against our own city names. **Never match places by name.** backlog.py matches on distance, which no language can fool, and it prints what is online, what would be a new page and what would deepen a live one.
- **Read this file's own history before researching a city.** CURATION.md records what was already tried and found empty. Cork was researched from zero three separate times before anyone checked; that is three windows spent on one city. **CURATION.md and LOG.md now hold one week; everything older sits in `archive/`, moved weekly by `scripts/archive_logs.py` and never deleted** (Hidde, 2026-08-08, on token cost: the two files had reached 1.6 MB in 24 days, and are 173 KB after). So the check is `grep -ri "<place>" CURATION.md archive/`, not CURATION.md alone: absence from the living file is not evidence a hunt is new.
- **Dispatch the search wide, verify narrow.** Candidate discovery parallelises well (one agent per city, or per park list); verification does not, because it is the part that must not be rushed. Two cities researched in parallel and verified carefully beats one city done end to end.
- **Photo hunting has a floor and a ceiling.** Hunt hard while a city is being written, then stop: a documented dead end in CURATION.md is a finished piece of work, and re-running an exhausted hunt is the single most repeated waste in this project's history.
- **Commit the smallest complete step first.** A city with four verified trees committed beats eight unverified in a session that gets cut off.
- **Never re-litigate a decision.** DECISIONS.md exists so a run can look up an answer in seconds instead of reasoning it out again.
- **The measured burn list (sessie 2026-08-09, uit twee dagen kostendata):** three kinds of pass are no longer dispatched without a specific new angle, because the meter says they buy nothing. (1) Photo hunts on sources flagged `exhausted` in data/photo-queue.json: the Commons/iNaturalist lane is measured DONE for the rollout cities at ~150k tokens per approval; new photos come from readers, partners, national aerial/open archives or a genuinely new source. (2) From-zero web-research cities without an explicit owner ask. (3) Pin passes without a register coordinate, aerial-imagery angle (BRIEF_RESEARCH.md) or equivalent concrete evidence path. The cheap end of the meter, always preferred: register imports (~0.4k/tree), the Wikidata candidates feed (0 tokens, printed in every brief), aerial pin checks (~2-5k), and dendrological-society registers and yearbooks (the dendron.dk playbook).

### Step 2: Verify each tree
For each candidate tree, cross-reference at least 2 independent sources for:
- Existence and species
- Approximate age
- Exact location (GPS coordinates)
If sources conflict or only one source exists, include the tree but set `curation_status: "flagged"` with a note explaining the uncertainty.

**A missing age is fine, and so is an approximate one (Hidde, 2026-08-10: "not having the age is fine, or approximate is fine, stop being so anal about these details").** This is the same correction he made about dead trees on 2026-08-06 and about `best_time` on 2026-08-02, so treat it as the settled pattern rather than a one-off: a gap in a nice-to-have field is not a reason to hold a tree back, to hedge the prose into mush, or to write paragraphs of apology about it. A tree with a spectacular 33-metre crown and no recorded age is a good entry. Leave `age_estimate` empty or state the range loosely, flag it, and move on.

**And an ESTIMATE is allowed, wanted, and is not the same thing as an invention (Hidde, 2026-08-16: "leeftijd is geen blocker - we kunnen een estimation doen", and, asked to make it permanent, "en onthou dit voor alle toekomstige runs").** This is recorded here rather than in a note because the note would not be read: it is the fourth time in a week that a run has treated an honest gap as a wall.

The distinction, and it is the whole rule: **an estimate is DERIVED and says what from; an invention is not.** Girth plus a published growth rate for that species is dendrology, and it is how every register in the world dates a tree. A documented planting date for the specimen is an estimate. A source that says "several hundred years" is an estimate we may repeat. What is none of those is a number that feels right, and that stays forbidden under hard rule 2 whatever it is called.

So a derived age ships with three properties. It states its basis, in `verify_notes` for the writer and in the prose for the reader ("about five metres round, which puts it near four hundred years"). It is BROAD, because the honest output of a growth-rate calculation is a century-wide band and narrowing it to look confident is the same error as a faked pin. And it never sharpens a vague source into a precise number: "several hundred years" may not become "roughly 350".

**The trap sits exactly here, so name it while estimating: the bridge claim (see Step 3).** "The park was laid out in 1864, therefore this linden is 160 years old" joins two true facts into a claim neither source makes, and the park's own planting date is the single most tempting bridge there is, because it is always available and always plausible. It only works when a source says THIS TREE belongs to that planting. Otherwise the two facts stay two facts, and the story may say the tree stands in a garden replanted in 1864 without saying it dates from it.

**Where no basis exists at all, the field stays empty and the PAGE ASKS THE READER**, which is the rule immediately below and is strictly better than a guess: somebody with a tape measure answers it in one message, and now we have a correspondent instead of a number. Empty still beats invented; empty no longer beats derived.

What does NOT loosen, because it is the honesty rule rather than a completeness rule: never invent an age to fill the field, and never sharpen a vague one into a precise one. The line is fabrication, not incompleteness.

**The missing input, and it is the real work: a couple of hundred published trees carry no age, and the girth that would let one be derived is rarer still (`python3 scripts/stats.py`).** Nothing can be derived from nothing, so an estimate is unavailable for almost every tree that needs one, and that is a data gap rather than a judgement gap. Girth is the cheap unlock: it is measurable from a photograph in a pinch, readers volunteer it, and several registers publish it (Amsterdam's `trunk_girth`, Piemonte's `girth_cm`, ICNF's metres-not-centimetres column). Capture `girth_cm` whenever a source gives it, even when the tree already has an age, because the day there are a few hundred of them the estimating becomes a script that runs over all of them at once instead of a judgement made one tree at a time. **Capture `height_m` on the same terms (2026-08-21):** from a register's own survey or a plaque, never from our own prose, because a height sentence in a story is as often about the species, the neighbour, or the tree before a storm took its top off. `scripts/heights.py` fills it from any register we have already imported, matching within 5 metres of our pin, or within 30 with the genus agreeing; re-run it after importing a register. Both fields feed the generated rankings at /collections/thickest-trees and /collections/tallest-trees, which re-rank themselves on every build, so a measurement added tonight moves the list tomorrow.

**If you do not know, publish it and ask the reader (Hidde, 2026-08-13).** On being shown that we refuse a tree when nobody can pin the exact species, four oaks in Nuremberg among them: "this one you can definitely put live - just put out the question for the user. and you can remember this - if you dont know just put live and put the question for the users."

So an unknown becomes a QUESTION ON THE PAGE, never a reason to hold the tree back. Write what is known ("Oak (Quercus sp.)", "species not established"), say plainly on the page that we do not know which one and invite the reader to tell us, and ship. The same applies to any field of this kind: an age nobody has measured, a girth nobody has taped, a planting date nobody recorded. This is the collecting mechanism the site already runs on, applied to gaps instead of only to errors, and it is strictly better than silence: an honest question invites a correction, while an omission invites nothing.

The one field this never applies to is location, for the reason it always is: a reader can answer "which oak is this" from the pavement, and cannot answer "why am I standing in the wrong place".

**And the general form, because the specific version got dodged within the hour (Hidde, 2026-08-10: "can you never ever stop a tree from going live if you're not sure about the size, it doesn't matter at all if you are wrong").** Told that a missing age must not hold a tree back, a run in the same session held four trees back on girth instead: below Japan's 300 cm giant-tree threshold, therefore perhaps not remarkable enough. Same instinct, different field, and naming the first field only invited the substitution. So, stated once in the form that closes all of them:

**A judgement call about whether a tree is good enough NEVER blocks publication.** Not size, not age, not fame, not how many sources agree, not whether it is the best tree in its park, and not its position in a queue: "the city is already at its target" and "this was ninth in line" are not reasons, they are the count doctrine misread (see "The count follows the trees" above). Every one of those is correctable after the fact by an edit or a reader, and being wrong about them costs nothing. Publish, flag what is thin, move on.

The list of things that DO stop a tree going live is short, closed, and none of it is a matter of taste: the hard rules (never fabricate, never publish a tree known to be dead, never a photo without a verified open licence, never a withheld or private location under hard rule 10), and a location that cannot be stated honestly. That is the whole list. A run that wants to hold a tree back for any other reason is applying its own taste as if it were a rule, which is the failure mode this file has now recorded four times in one day.

### Step 3: Write the stories
150-250 words per tree (the Paris run of 2026-07-15 sets the standard; anything over 250 words gets shortened). Style rules:
- Direct, specific, slightly vivid. Scott Galloway meets nature writing.
- Lead with the most surprising fact.
- Include what the tree has "witnessed" historically.
- Never use: "hidden gem", "must-see", "breathtaking", "nestled".
- Never use em dashes.

**Never join two sourced facts into a claim neither source makes (the bridge claim, 2026-08-13).** This is the failure mode that actually produces invented stories here, and it does not feel like inventing anything, which is why it needs naming. Baarn's Pekingtuin is the worked example: Wikipedia says an American oak in that park was planted around 1850 and is 5.52 metres round, a newspaper says a ring carrying a poem was placed around a tree in that park, and our story said the ring marks off the oak's root plate. Both sources true, the sentence joining them false, and the pin followed the sentence to a different tree sixty metres away. Every fact checked out and the page was still wrong.

So, while writing: when a sentence asserts that two things are the same thing, or that A is because of B, ask which single source says so. If none does, either drop the join or write the two facts as two facts. The prose is allowed to be less satisfying; a story that reads as a neat explanation is exactly what this error produces, because a coherent narrative is what a writer reaches for when the evidence stops short. And note where the damage lands: the join usually decides the LOCATION, which is the one field a reader cannot forgive us for (see "the one field that carries the product"). The pin half of this is enforced by `check_pin_upgrades()` in scripts/preflight.py, which refuses a pin upgraded to confirmed when one of our own pin passes recorded it as approximate and no source names new evidence. The prose half is a question you have to ask yourself.

Stories and any page copy must satisfy SEO_GEO_BLUEPRINT.md, in particular P2 (answer first, in the first two sentences where the page is a question/city page) and P3 (unique content, no fill-in-the-city-name templating), and follow TONE_OF_VOICE.md, including its calibration examples and hard bans.

**Every tree carries a year calendar (Hidde, 2026-08-02: "elke boom een mooi overzicht met bladloos, blad, verkleuring, fruit, bloem, en die moeten gewoon kloppen").** The calendar shows the whole year, bare / in leaf / flowers / fruit / autumn colour, with the current month marked.

**What the curve draws, ruled by Hidde 2026-08-05: how much there is to SEE, not leaf cover.** The first version drew leaf cover, which made every evergreen a straight line, and a straight line says nothing ("nu, als het een groen blad heeft, dan is die lijn gewoon recht. Dat zegt ook niets"). So each moment carries a judgement in `intensity` on the species file, one of `unseen`, `nice`, `striking`, `worth the trip`, and that judgement is what gives the curve its height. A ginkgo flowers in April and nobody can tell; it turns gold in November and people cross town. `nice` is the default, deliberately the dull one, so a species nobody has judged yet gets no false peak. `worth the trip` is scarce for the same reason `best_time` is: 8 of 25 species carry one. Curves are never normalised per species, so an uneventful tree stays low, and a species where nothing rises above `unseen` gets no chart at all, only a plain sentence. The build refuses a species that records real moments and still draws a flat line. `best_time` is unchanged and now lives inside this chart as its marked peak, which is also where the "at its best right now" chip sits: the tree page used to stack two curve charts making the same promise.

It lives per species in `data/phenology/[species-slug].json`, not per tree, because phenology is a property of the species in a climate rather than a fact about an individual. The build shifts the months by latitude (a month early below 42N, a month late above 56N) and prints nothing at all below 25N, because the temperate pattern is simply wrong in the tropics and a wrong calendar is worse than none. Each phase may carry a short label saying what actually happens ("acorns drop, and a good mast year buries the ground in them"). The page states plainly that the weeks shift with the year.

A species with no file shows no calendar: an honest gap, exactly like a missing photo. 25 species covering 302 tree pages exist as of 2026-08-02; adding the rest is normal run work, oldest-species-first by tree count. Every entry needs a real source, and evergreens get an empty bare row rather than an invented one.

**Priority note, Hidde 2026-08-08: season data is the lowest-priority depth item, below photos.** His words: "don't waste too much time on finding the season of the tree, it's less important than photos and we can do it later." Setting `best_time` while writing a story, from a species file that already exists, stays free and welcome. Researching a species' phenology from scratch is not worth a research window in the current phase. Everything below still governs what a `best_time` may say when one is set; none of it is loosened, it is simply not chased.

**Set `best_time` when, and only when, a tree has a real seasonal peak.** Broadened by Hidde on 2026-08-02, because the first reading was too narrow: a peak is anything a visitor would notice and enjoy on the day, not only a burst of colour. Three families count:
- **Flower, leaf and colour**: blossom, autumn colour, catkins, fresh leaves. The original reading.
- **Fruit, nuts and mast**: acorns underfoot in October, conkers, sweet chestnuts you can gather, figs, the olive harvest, mulberries. Hidde: "deze boom heeft nog steeds vruchten, eikels, kastanjes, op een moment wat leuk is om te weergeven." Use `kind: fruit`.
- **Bare winter structure**: a veteran tree with real architecture is at its most legible in January, when the leaves are gone and the whole frame of it shows. Hidde: "geen bladeren in de winter wat leuk is om te weergeven." Use `kind: bare silhouette`. Only for trees whose shape is actually the point: a hollow, buttressed or wildly limbed veteran, never a young street tree.

Two limits keep this honest. **A tree gets one `best_time`, so pick its strongest moment**, and **scarcity is what makes the badge mean anything**: if every deciduous tree claimed both an autumn fruit peak and a winter silhouette, the "at its best right now" badge would light up permanently and stop being a reason to go anywhere. Give it to the trees where the moment is genuinely worth the trip and leave the rest empty.
 This is the single strongest reason a page gives someone to actually go, because it turns "nice" into "this weekend". A tree with a pronounced moment gets:

```json
"best_time": { "months": [11], "label": "late November, when the ginkgo turns gold" }
```

`months` is the list of month numbers when it is at its best and drives the "at its best right now" badge; `label` is the short phrase a reader sees, in the tone of voice. Set `kind` too (Hidde, 2026-07-29, the PictureThis point: say WHY it peaks): one of `flowers`, `fruit`, `autumn colour`, `catkins`, `fresh leaves`; it renders as an icon chip on the season block. The build derives a kind from unambiguous label words when the field is missing, and shows nothing when in doubt; an explicit field always beats derivation. Rules:
- Only add it when the moment is real and specific: blossom, autumn colour, a wingnut's summer catkins, a magnolia's ten days. An evergreen or an ancient yew that looks much the same all year gets no `best_time` at all. Forcing one on every tree is filler and defeats the point.
- Base it on the species and the local climate, and say what actually happens in the label, not just a month. "May, when it flowers" beats "May".
- Keep it honest: if a peak spans a range, use the real months. Guessing a precise week you cannot support is the same mistake as a fake pin.

### Step 4: Find photos
Search Wikimedia Commons and other openly-licensed sources (CC0, CC-BY, CC-BY-SA only). Record the exact license and attribution. If no good photo exists, set photo status to `missing`. NEVER use photos from monumentaltrees.com, Google Maps, or any source without a clear open license.

**Every rendered image goes through `img_srcset()` (the image standard, Hidde 2026-07-31: "kunnen we deze image handling standaard maken").** thumb_url() maps Wikimedia (fixed buckets, capped at 960), Unsplash and iNaturalist to right-sized variants; scripts/qa.py fails the deploy if any img tag ships a full-resolution original, so a new render site cannot skip the helper unnoticed.

**Which photo, the Cadiz standard (Hidde, 2026-07-31: "alle fotos bij Cadiz zijn goed, je ziet goed de boom, niet te ver en niet te dicht ingezoomd, genoeg licht").** Cadiz is the calibration set for photo selection, the way Paris is for tone. A photo qualifies when: (1) the tree is unmistakably the subject and fills most of the frame, crown and trunk both readable; (2) it is neither a distant view where the tree has to be searched for, nor a close-up of bark or leaves; (3) where there is a choice between usable candidates, a tree IN LEAF beats a bare one (Hidde, 2026-08-11: "ik zou altijd voorkeur geven voor een boom met blad ipv zonder als je de keuze hebt"); a leafless crown reads as dormant, and the product is a good afternoon outside. This is a tiebreaker, never a rejection reason: a bare photograph still beats no photograph, and the `bare silhouette` best_time is the deliberate exception. (4) it is taken in daylight, properly exposed and in colour, which is now measured rather than remembered (`python3 scripts/photo_light.py <file-or-url>` prints brightness, contrast, colour and blown highlights, and says POOR, WEAK or OK; Hidde, 2026-08-13, on the Weichselboom going live backlit: "deze foto heeft wel echt slechte belichting bijna zwart wit... kun je belichting beoordeling meenemen in je foto keuze". Run it on a shortlist before looking, and let a POOR score end the matter; a WEAK one still ships when it is the only photograph of that tree, which is the Weichselboom's own case): never a night shot, and never black-and-white or archival imagery, however atmospheric (Hidde, 2026-07-31: "liever geen foto dan zwart-wit"); a 1904 stereograph says museum, not go-there-this-afternoon; (5) it survives the card crop: pages crop images to wide cards, so the CENTRE band of the image must still show the tree, not the buildings beside it (the Falcone Tree took three attempts because rounds one and two failed exactly this). And the rule that makes the rest enforceable: **LOOK at the image before approving it. Render the actual file. Never approve from a filename, a description or a thumbnail guess; if tooling cannot display images this run, the photo stays `found_needs_check` for a run that can.** Which is not a hypothetical, though the reason recorded here was wrong twice over and is corrected 2026-09-01: the runner reaches `upload.wikimedia.org`, its 500px and 960px buckets, `api.openverse.org` and iNaturalist, all 200 OK, so a viewing pass runs anywhere and the 2026-08-07 verdict that it could not is retired (the failures were 400s on an unserved thumbnail width). What survives from that whole episode is the one true half: Wikimedia does not block us, it RATE-LIMITS. A burst of 29 image fetches got HTTP 429 after twelve; roughly one request every three seconds runs clean. A viewing pass throttles, and the CI problem is a proxy question rather than a source question. Read that honestly, because for a long time it was not true: `found_needs_check` **renders on the site**, so 26 photographs nobody had looked at were live when this was discovered on 2026-08-06. That stays the behaviour, because holding a page's only image back over a risk a reader can report contradicts the same day's ruling on dead trees. What it needs, and now has, is a third status: **`held` keeps a photo in the file and off the site**, for the case where showing it would be wrong rather than merely unchecked, such as a good photograph that might be of the wrong tree. Margaret Island in Budapest is the worked example: two excellent images of "a very large plane tree", on an island with three separately named notable planes, so attaching one would be a guess dressed as a fact. A tree with no qualifying photo keeps an honest gap; a mediocre photo is not a rough version of a good one. Hidde restated this on 2026-08-08, and his wording is the clearest the project has: "I don't think making a random picture of a tree to fill it up is a good idea, better nothing than that." So a photograph ships only when it is OF THIS TREE and meets the standard. Never a generic photograph of the species, never a nice view of the park it stands in, never a stock image dressed as a record. The species silhouette that a photo-less tree page carries is not an exception to this: it is line art, obviously a drawing rather than a photograph, and it appears beside a sentence saying in plain words that no photograph of this tree exists. If it ever starts reading as a substitute for the real thing, remove it rather than soften the rule.

**Credits: record always, display only when the licence requires it (Hidde, 2026-07-29).** CC BY and BY-SA photos keep their visible on-page credit, that is the licence's price and it never gets stripped; if a visible credit has to go, the photo changes to a source that needs none (Unsplash License), never the other way round. Decorative and marketing imagery (hero, app page) comes from no-credit-needed sources from the start.

**A photograph a reader sends through the app is publishable, ruled by Hidde 2026-09-02 ("ook als de boom er al is en geen foto heeft of de foto van de gebruiker is beter gaan we die gebruiken - onthou dit").** Both camera paths feed one pool: a photograph attached to a tree we do not map yet, and one taken while ticking off a tree we already map. If the page has no photograph, or the reader's is better by the Cadiz standard, it ships, credited to their display name and never to an email address. This does not loosen the standard itself: look at the pixels, and a photograph of the wrong trunk is still the wrong trunk.

The condition is the whole safety of it: **nothing is published until the app says so at the moment somebody sends one.** Today `describeForm` in CollectSheet.swift still reads "Your photograph is yours and nobody else sees it", and a photograph given under that sentence is not covered by a rule written after it. The new line travels with the next build; the full record, including the deletion promise, is DECISIONS.md 2026-09-02.

**The owner is not a content source (Hidde, 2026-07-30: "stop met het mij vragen fotos te maken").** A run never asks Hidde to photograph, visit or field-verify anything; one owner does not scale and it is not his job. The supply ladder for a photo gap is: open-licence sources (below), then reader submissions (UGC, the flywheel), and otherwise the gap stays an honest gap in CURATION.md. AI imagery is allowed ONLY as clearly-labeled illustration (the parked dummy-image direction, BACKLOG.md); an AI-generated image presented as a photo of a real, specific tree is fabrication under hard rule 2 and never ships. Any paid generation tool needs his yes first (hard rule 5).

**Do not stop at Wikimedia.** Coverage there varies by country rather than by tree, which is why Tokyo came in at 7 photos out of 10 and London at 0. Same trees, different places to look. When Wikimedia comes up empty, try in this order:
- **iNaturalist**, which often has CC-licensed photographs with coordinates attached, useful for confirming a pin as well as illustrating it. Check the individual observation's licence, it varies per photo.
- **Flickr**, filtered to CC licences. Old and well photographed trees are often there under CC-BY.
- **Openverse**, which searches many open repositories at once.
- **The city or park authority's own site**, where the licence is sometimes explicitly open.

A photo does more for goal 1 than any paragraph, because it is what makes someone decide the walk is worth it. Hunt hard, but per the MVP mindset, never hold a city back for one.

### The register layer (Hidde's two-layer model, approved 2026-07-29/30)

Above the curated cities sits a second, wider layer: **officially designated remarkable trees from government registers, shown as honestly-labeled dots on the map.** Hidde's framing: the app that shows all monumental trees becomes "the first connected tree register of the world", with our curated selection of the most epic trees on top. Layer 2 is coverage and demand-sensing; layer 1 stays the product. Rules for runs, all binding:

- **One official register counts as the source** for a layer-2 dot ("ik vertrouw de overheid wel"); the two-independent-sources bar keeps applying to everything in layer 1.
- **Semantic filter, never bulk import:** only trees a register itself designates as monumental/remarkable. Never full municipal tree inventories. Hard rule 10 applies in full: access-filtered, withheld or blurred locations respected, private land excluded.
- **Licence first:** a register is usable only with a verified open licence compatible with commercial-leaning reuse (CC BY, PDDL 1.0, 政府標準利用規約 2.0, PDL 1.0). Non-commercial terms (MLIT P32, the Texas and Nevada big-tree registries) disqualify a source outright, though such a source may still be used to FIND and cross-check trees that are then hand-verified from elsewhere. Record the proving sentence in OPEN_DATA_SURVEY.md before importing anything.

  **And re-read a licence before trusting a verdict about it, because one of ours was wrong for months and it was the most expensive kind of wrong: a NO that closed a door nobody reopened.** This line used to name the NL national register as non-commercial and therefore disqualified. It is not. The Landelijk Register Monumentale Bomen states `Onder bronvermelding te gebruiken: (c)Bomenstichting` on every dataset in its own DCAT catalogue at monumentalebomen.nl/data.json, read 2026-08-18: attribution required, no non-commercial clause. The register holds 16,094 trees and is the richest we have anywhere. Beside species, coordinates and a planting band it carries `visitable` and `visible`, which answers hard rule 10 from the data instead of from a search, an owner category, and a written history per tree. Either the terms changed with the v2024 release or the original scouting read the wrong page; either way the verdict outlived the fact. A licence verdict is evidence with a date on it, so re-check one that blocks a whole country before treating it as settled.
- **Honesty over polish:** register coordinates are often area-level; imported dots default to `location_precision: approximate` unless verified. Dots carry the label "from the official register, not yet verified by us", get NO own pages (thin-page risk) and are NOT collectible; the tier/game design is parked in BACKLOG.md until the mid-September checkpoint, and a run may not build any of it.
- **Pilot: Japan, riding with the wave.** Scouted sources and verdicts live in OPEN_DATA_SURVEY.md ("Japan scouting", 2026-07-30): Tokyo's CC BY CSV with coordinates is the first import candidate, the national Natural Monument register is the backbone (read its /top/policy in full first), Kyushu/Hokuriku prefectures still need scouting. Build order for runs: finish the scouting, then the data file (data/registers/), then the explore-map layer, smallest honest step first.

### Step 0b: Process reader submissions (before picking a new city)

Readers submit trees and whole cities through a public form. Hidde is deliberately not in this loop: submissions come straight to you.

Submissions arrive in the `submissions` table of Hidde's Supabase (the native form on /contribute, live since 2026-07-31). A run reads them ONLY when the SUPABASE_SERVICE_KEY env var is present (curl GET {SUPABASE_URL}/rest/v1/submissions with apikey+Bearer set to it); when the secret is absent, skip without complaint. Track processed rows by id in `data/submissions-processed.json`, same as always.

The old channel (a `SUBMISSIONS_CSV_URL` constant, a published-CSV Google Sheet, once read as a fallback) retired along with scripts/build_site.py when it was deleted 2026-08-09: that constant lived nowhere else, and the Supabase table above has been the live channel since 2026-07-31. Nothing to fetch from a CSV any more.

For each new submission:
1. Treat it as a research lead, never as fact. A submitter saying a tree is 500 years old is a claim to verify, not a source.
2. Apply the exact same bar as your own research: two independent sources for existence, species and age, and a location you can place precisely. The hard rules still apply in full, especially "never fabricate" and "photos need a verified open licence".
3. If it verifies: write the story in the tone of voice and add it to the right city file. **Never put the sender's name on the site.** Ruled by Hidde 2026-08-11 ("privacy technisch echt een no go... ookal is het via formulier niet meer doen nooit") after a run rendered a submitter's name on a tree page. It applies whatever the channel: an email is obviously not consent, and a name typed into our own form is not consent either, because people fill a field without expecting to be published and a name plus a city plus a tree they visit is personal data we have no business exposing. Thank them privately instead; that is what keeps a contributor coming back. If the city does not exist yet, create it as a new city file with the submitted trees, and set its status to `needs_curation`.

   The `submitted_by` field survives in the schema so older files still validate, and nothing renders it. Do not write new ones. Photograph credits are a different thing and stay: a CC BY licence obliges us to name the photographer, and they chose that licence knowing it.
4. If it does not verify: do not publish it. Record it in CURATION.md under the submission's city with what is missing, so Hidde or a later run can pick it up.
5. Either way, append the row id to `data/submissions-processed.json` so it is never handled twice, and note the outcome in CURATION.md.

**Closing the loop (Hidde, 2026-08-21: input is core and never dismissed).** After verifying a submission, the run records what happened and, when the reader left an email, writes their answer:

- Set `outcome` on the row via the service key (PATCH `/rest/v1/submissions?id=eq.N`): `changed`, `holds`, or `open_question`.
- When the row is answerable, compose `reply_text` on the row: one short specific paragraph from the verification record. Answerable means it carries a `user_id` (feedback is account-gated since 2026-08-21, the Google Maps convention Hidde chose after asking for the competitor check: the options are visible to everyone, acting needs sign-in, and the account is the reply channel; a privacy request is the one ungated kind) or, on older rows, a typed `email`. `changed` says what changed, with the page link. `holds` shows the work and ends in a question back, never "you were wrong": the reader stood there and we did not. `open_question` asks for the specific missing piece (which tree, a photo, a girth). Every reply ends by inviting the answer through /contribute (link prefilled with the tree) and more input generally. `scripts/contributor_reply.py` transports it after mailcheck passes; the run never sends mail itself.
- A `could not tell which tree` report (the chip added 2026-08-21 because three of the first four real reports were exactly this: Toulouse's two elms, Prague's island oak, Helsinki's unphotographed pine) is never a pin question. Its fix is a recognition line in the story (what the trunk, bark or setting looks like that the neighbours do not), a trunk photo, or both; and the answer mail asks the reader which tree they examined, because that is the one fact they hold and we do not.
- A row whose `why` starts with `report detail:` is the reader's answer to the chip's follow-up question (asked inline since 2026-08-21; the chip row arrived minutes earlier from the same account on the same tree). Read the two together as one report; the detail is usually the fact that settles it.
- Rows whose `why` starts with `vote undone` are bookkeeping from the thumbs' undo: mark them processed, verify nothing, mail nothing.
- A second report on an already-checked tree reopens the question rather than being waved off with the earlier verdict.
- Log the exchange in the tree's `verify_notes`, so the next run continues the thread instead of restarting it.

A submitted tree that verifies is worth more than a new city researched from scratch, because it proves someone cares about that city. Process submissions first, then continue with the next pending city if the usage window still allows.

## The mandate

This section exists so a run can decide for itself. Hidde is not watching, does not want to be the thing that starts the work, and will read `LOG.md` when he feels like it. Everything below is what he would say if you could ask him.

### What this is for

Two goals, deliberately ranked, and a third beneath them that works differently.

**1. Get someone to walk to a specific tree and enjoy standing in front of it.** Not "raise awareness of trees", not traffic. A named person, on a named street, looking up at a named tree that turned out to be worth the walk. Every feature, page and sentence is judged on whether it makes that more likely.

**2. Earn money.** Hidde does not need to get rich off this, but it should pay for itself and then some. (The "few thousand euro a month" figure that used to stand here was called loose talk by Hidde himself on 2026-07-26, GO_TO_MARKET.md "the revenue arithmetic: parked"; treat the amount as undecided, the direction as real.)

When the two conflict, goal 1 wins, because goal 2 does not exist without it.

**3. Become the richest database of trees worth visiting (Hidde, 2026-08-14), and it is a tiebreaker, not a master.** Subordinate on his own ruling, because a database without visitors is MonumentalTrees, the competitor we beat on experience. It binds three habits rather than ordering any work: when two ways of doing something are otherwise equal, choose the one that leaves the database richer; never store a bare fact, store its provenance with it, because provenance is what makes a reader's correction mergeable; and never let the schema assume "city", since cities are the front door while villages, parks and forests must later be new views on the same data. The user half is the synergy loop he named the same day: we set it up, readers validate and correct it, and the "Worth the visit?" control plus the publish-and-ask rule are its engines. Full record in DECISIONS.md 2026-08-14; it licenses no database features ahead of demand.

**The two phases, named by Hidde 2026-08-12. Phase 1 is traction on the website: collecting people and data. Phase 2 is the app and monetisation.** We are in phase 1 and there is no date on the switch; evidence moves it, not the calendar.

This is the sharpest filter the project has, because it turns the drift trigger from a philosophical question into an answerable one. **Does this piece of work collect people, collect data, or win traction?** If none of the three, it is phase 2 and it waits, however good it is.

- **People** are readers, contributors, correspondents, the waitlist: anyone who might send a tree, a photograph or a link. The outreach threads are phase 1 work of the highest kind, because a reply is a person.
- **Data** is trees, registers, photographs, coordinates. A register import is phase 1; so is a reader's tip.
- **Traction** is impressions, clicks, and somebody linking to us. Measured in DATA.md, never in trees published.

What this does NOT change: hard rules 1 and 2 stay shut. Accounts and payment are phase 2 by definition, and the recorded sequence in "Where this is going" is unchanged, so nothing here reopens them. The four verbs stay the product; phase 1 is how the product gets an audience, not a different product.

The honest use of it is on our own work. On 2026-08-11 hours went into walk chips, a pin badge and mail wording. The mails were phase 1 and were the right call. The chips and the badge were neither people, nor data, nor traction. Nobody subscribes to a map that sent them to the wrong place.

Note what that does and does not imply. It does not mean polish before coverage: a map of five cities cannot get anyone outside who does not live in those five. It means never lying about what you have. A rough pin that admits it is rough still gets someone to the right park, and they can enjoy the hunt. A rough pin dressed up as exact gets them to a spot where the tree is not, and they do not come back. Build wide, label honestly.

**The bar under both: the experience has to be good.** MonumentalTrees has more trees than this project ever will, and it does not matter, because using it is miserable. That is the entire opening. So a page that is accurate but ugly, slow, confusing or joyless has not met the standard, even though every fact checks out.

Read that as a floor, not as a reason to stop and polish. The existing page template already clears it: a story worth reading, a map, a working directions button, nearby trees, an honest note when the pin is rough. Pouring a new city into that template gives you a good experience by default, so coverage and quality are not in conflict here. They only conflict when someone proposes rebuilding the template, and the answer to that is in the section above.

### Reason from distribution first (Hidde, 2026-08-09)

**The binding constraint on this project is not supply, it is distribution, and every decision about what to work on gets made through that lens until the numbers say otherwise.** His words: "we hebben volgens mij een distributie probleem."

The measurement behind it, so this is a finding and not a mood. In the 28 days to 2026-08-09 the whole site took 78 clicks on 3,680 impressions, with zero external referrers and, as Ahrefs showed the same day, 298 referring domains of which Ahrefs flags every one as spam. Not statistics pages, which was this session's first and wrong reading: an SEO link network (itxoft-professional-seo-services.site, fiverr-seo-for-small-businesses.site, rankgrowth.agency and 295 more), all first seen from 24 July 2026, the fortnight the domain began to exist. Nobody bought them; these networks spray new registrations. Every link is nofollow, so the direct ranking effect is close to nil, but the practical consequence stands: the backlink COUNT is meaningless as a measure here and the number of referring domains a human chose to create is zero. The meter is Domain Rating and referring domains above DR 10 that are not flagged spam. Rome, the deepest page on the site at 24 trees, took 61 impressions and **zero** clicks. Lisbon, with fewer trees, took 8 clicks. Porto, which appears in no phase of the rollout, took 4 more. Two of 95 cities produced 15 percent of every click. Meanwhile supply sits idle by the thousand: 555 registered Portuguese trees, 511 register clusters, leads files in every city.

Read those two facts together and the conclusion is unavoidable. **Depth does not produce visitors. Position does, and we hold position only where the competition is thin.** We rank second for a small Dutch town's oldest tree and nowhere for Rome's, because everybody writes about Rome and nobody writes about Baarn. A page nobody can find is not a rough version of a page people find; it is a different thing, and doing it beautifully does not change that.

What this changes about how a run decides, in order:

- **Before doing the work, say what will bring anyone to it.** For a city that means two things together, never one: real demand (`python3 scripts/demand.py`, English Wikipedia pageviews as the proxy, validated against our own Search Console impressions) AND a reason to believe we can rank, which usually means nobody else has written the page. Big and contested loses to medium and empty.
- **Work that gives somebody a reason to link to us outranks work that adds another page nobody links to.** Zero backlinks is the ceiling under every ranking on this site, and no amount of trees lifts it.
- **Depth is for pages that already get impressions.** Deepening a page with demand is compounding; deepening a page with none is the most expensive way to do nothing. Search Console says which is which, and a run may act on that alone (see "Search evidence is a run's to act on").
- **Measure output in impressions and clicks, not in trees published.** A week that added 40 trees and no visibility was a week of inventory, and inventory is not progress here.
- **Most distribution actions need Hidde**, because a run never writes as him (hard rule 4). So a run's half of this is building the thing worth linking to and leaving the draft ready; the sending is his.

Two limits, both firm. This changes the ORDER of work and never the standard: the never-cuttable list, the hard rules and the research bar are untouched, and a distribution argument is never a reason to publish something thinner or less honest. And it expires on evidence, like everything else here: the day backlinks exist and pages rank on their merits, supply becomes the constraint again and this section should be rewritten rather than obeyed out of habit.

### Ship rough, ship wide, fix later

This project is unproven. Nobody has walked to a tree because of it, and nobody has paid for anything. Until that changes, the job is to put a lot of usable material in front of people quickly and find out whether any of it lands. Polish is what you do to something people already use.

**The default is the roughest version that is honest and useful.** Before any piece of work, ask: what is the smallest version that gets one person to a tree, or tells us whether anyone cares? Do that, ship it, move on.

Two lists, and the difference between them is the whole idea.

**The one field that carries the product is location (Hidde, 2026-08-10).** His words: "location is the only real important part about a tree. It may be dead, wrong photo, or wrong age. These things can be adjusted. We want to strive for good data but 95% to start with is fine." This ranks the fields, which nothing here had done: a wrong age is an edit, a wrong photo is a swap, a tree that turns out to have died is a removal, and all three are things a reader can tell us and we can fix in minutes. A wrong location is different in kind, because the person is already standing in the wrong place and the promise is broken before any correction can reach them. It is the same reasoning as hard rule 2 ("a wrong location kills user trust permanently"), now stated as a priority rather than only as a prohibition.

So: 95 percent is the shipping standard, not 100, and the missing 5 percent should fall on the adjustable fields rather than on the pin. Chase pin quality; let age, photo and the rest be corrected later by readers, who are the correction layer everywhere else on this site.

Two things this does NOT license, and both matter because the sentence is easy to over-read in the other direction. It is a tolerance for ERROR, not permission to publish what we already know to be wrong: a tree known to be dead still never ships, and one reported dead is still checked and removed the same day (his own "we doen niet aan dode bomen" stands). And it never loosens the location rules themselves, which run the opposite way: precision stays optional, honesty about precision stays absolute, and hard rule 10 on withheld and private locations is untouched. Read plainly, this raises the bar on pins by lowering it everywhere else.

**Always cuttable, without discussion:**
- Precision. An approximate pin that says so is a finished first version.
- A documented age. Missing or approximate is fine and needs no apology; see Step 2. Only inventing one is forbidden.
- Photo coverage. Hunt hard for photos, they do more for goal 1 than any paragraph, but never hold a city back for them.
- Completeness. Eight good trees ship. The ninth can come later.
- Consistency and polish across pages, matching styles, tidy edge cases.
- A third and fourth source once two independent ones already agree.

**Never cuttable, at any speed:**
- Never fabricate. An invented fact is not a rough version of a real one.
- Never fake precision. Vague is fine when it says it is vague. See the honesty rule in Step 0.
- Never publish a photo without a verified open licence and attribution.
- Never promise the visitor something that is not there.
- Never publish a tree whose location its source deliberately withholds, or one on private land that is not open to visitors (hard rule 10). Speed is never bought from a tree's safety or someone's front garden.

Speed is bought from the first list only. Anything bought from the second is not a fast MVP, it is a broken product with a good excuse.

**The trap to watch for: a gate, threshold or floor that enforces polish before there is demand for it.** Ask what evidence says anyone is waiting on the other side; usually the honest answer is nobody, and the gate should not exist. It has already happened here once (the 2026-07-21 quality floor; the full story is PRINCIPLES.md #13).

Prefer work that produces a signal over work that improves something nobody has used. Hard rule 6 asks whether real people will pay; this section is how that question gets answered, by shipping enough for the question to be answerable at all.

None of this is permanent. It flips when there is evidence: real visitors, submissions arriving, someone paying. Then polish stops being premature and starts being the job.

### Walk the user's timeline before you build

Cheap, and it is not a gate: it costs a minute of thinking and blocks nothing. Before building anything a person keeps, returns to, or accumulates, say out loud what happens at each of these, and fix whatever the answer embarrasses you with:

- **The first minute.** This is the only part that gets tested by default, and it is almost never where the problem is.
- **Day seven, and day thirty.** Is it still there? Does it still make sense? Has it quietly grown wrong?
- **The second device.** They read on a laptop and walk with a phone. Does the thing follow them?
- **The gap.** They come back after three weeks away. What do they find, and is it what they left?
- **The moment it fails.** Not whether it can fail, but what the person loses when it does, and whether they saw it coming.

The lesson behind this list: knowing something does not help if nothing prompts you to ask. A feature whose value accrues over time has to be reasoned about over time, or the one property that matters is the one property never checked. (The worked example, the tree passport that Safari would have silently wiped, is PRINCIPLES.md #12.)

### How Hidde's incoming thoughts get processed (his own instruction, 2026-07-28)

He fires many thoughts, fast, and that is the point of him; the system's duty is the other half: **test every incoming idea against the recorded picture before absorbing it.** Concretely, for each thought: (1) check what CLAUDE.md, PRODUCT_IA.md, PRINCIPLES.md, DECISIONS.md and BACKLOG.md already say, (2) name in one line what it touches, strengthens or contradicts, (3) only then build, park or push back. An idea that contradicts the corpus is not wrong (he outranks the corpus), but the contradiction must be said out loud so changing course is a decision, never a drift. Speed comes after the check, not instead of it.

**The product-copy layer (Hidde, 2026-08-24: "kun je zorgen dat je een andere manier van schrijven gebruikt voor tekst voor in het product - kun je jezelf daar een laag overheen gooien?").** Before writing ANY string a reader sees in the app or on a control, read `PRODUCT_COPY.md`. It is one page and it exists because this corpus is written in short aphoristic sentences, which is the right register for recording a decision and the wrong one for telling somebody what they can do. The failing example was "Walk to it and photograph it. That is how a tree gets added.", which he answered with "You can add a tree by taking a photograph and adding details": the reader as the subject, the method joined with "by", and no summary line after it. `python3 scripts/copycheck.py` greps the app's strings for the five habits and found five more the moment it was written, which is why this is a layer rather than one correction. TONE_OF_VOICE.md is untouched and still governs stories and page copy; this is about the twenty words somebody reads while holding the thing.

**The convention check (Hidde, 2026-08-01: "als we een nieuwe functie maken dat je eerst een conventie onderzoek doet - anders zijn we elke keer het wiel zelf aan het uitvinden en dat wil ik niet").** Before building any new user-facing interaction, first look at how the best consumer products do exactly that interaction (AllTrails, Google Maps, Airbnb, PictureThis are the standing references) and adopt the convention rather than inventing one. Novelty in interaction patterns is a cost, not a feature; users arrive trained by the products they already use. The proof case: both search fields were homegrown type-and-submit forms with a native datalist that iOS renders as a broken QuickType strip; the convention (live suggestions under the field, tap goes straight there) replaced them the day this rule was set. The lesson is not about logos. It is that **caution and invention are not the same thing**, and reaching for the second because of the first is how a product ends up full of controls nobody recognises. Where no official asset exists, copy the reference product rather than designing one. ### Deciding on something nobody wrote a rule for

Most of what comes up is not in any list. Ask three questions, in order:

1. **Does it serve goal 1 or goal 2?** If neither, do not do it, however satisfying it looks. Activity is not progress.
2. **Can Hidde undo it?** Anything reversible is yours to decide. Ship it, write it in `LOG.md`, let him object afterwards. Being wrong in `LOG.md` is cheap.
3. **Can it hurt anyone other than Hidde?** Readers, submitters, people whose photos or data are involved. If yes, stop, whatever question 1 and 2 said. This is the only one that overrides the rest.

Design, layout, features, page structure, copy, iconography and content all pass this test routinely. Hidde has said explicitly that until the product is proven he would rather you moved than checked. Take him at his word.

## Quality gate: the research standard, not Hidde

## The learning loop, coded rather than remembered (Hidde, 2026-08-06)

## QA: four layers, ruled by Hidde 2026-07-29

The 2026-07-29 whole-product walk found a dead check-in button on all 345 tree pages, five broken photos and seven self-contradicting pages, all live. The lesson is structural, so QA is a standing work form:

1. **Build-time checks, every build.** The Astro build (`site/`, `npx astro build`; the Python renderer this once meant, build_site.py, was deleted 2026-08-09 after a clean deploy cycle from Astro) refuses contract violations, bare script text, File: page photo urls, and oldest-tree pages whose answer names a different tree than the page is built around. 2. **Machine gates on every push.** scripts/qa.py gates the deploy (internal links resolve, no em dashes or banned words in rendered text, image sources are real image urls); the separate Smoke test workflow renders key pages in headless Chrome and asserts the product works as executed (maps construct, buttons exist, no script source visible as text). A run's rung 2 includes checking that the latest "Smoke test" workflow run succeeded (`gh run list --workflow=smoke.yml -L 1`); a red smoke run means the site is broken.
3. **The fresh-eyes reviewer, daily.** A separate context with no builder memory reviews the last day's diffs and the built site against the corpus and writes findings to REVIEW.md (severity BLOCKER/WARN/NOTE), fixing nothing. Runs pick BLOCKERs up as rung 2 and WARNs as rung 3; Step 0 includes reading REVIEW.md's newest entry. **The per-change eyes rule (Hidde, 2026-08-14: "so you dont have eyes to check the UI you put live?").** A session shipping any user-facing change LOOKS at the changed element, rendered, in the browser, at 375px and desktop, before reporting it done. Every interaction bug Hidde caught on 2026-08-14 (a bar sized like a banner, a dialog parked at the top of the document, a dimmed tree that would not select) had shipped through a grep-verified deploy and would have been visible in one screenshot. Night runs cannot do it, which is already the rule (runs do no visual-taste work); a session has no such excuse.

3d. **The app is made to FAIL on purpose, since 2026-08-27, because everything above only ever ran the happy path.** Hidde: "ik heb niet het idee dat we de app heel actief aan het stress testen zijn qa testen etc." He was right, and the cause was structural: eighteen calls sat on `URLSession.shared` across twelve files, and URLSession.shared cannot be intercepted, so no test could take the network away from any of them. The whole class of failure a walking app actually meets was not untested, it was unreachable.

   - **One door: `Kit/Net.swift`.** Every network call goes through it. In a release build it is a pass-through to `URLSession.shared` and nothing else. `python3 scripts/netcheck.py` fails the iOS build on any call that goes round it, which it had to within hours: two files that landed on main the same afternoon carried nine calls written against the old habit, and nothing said so.

2. NEVER fabricate tree facts. If you cannot verify age or location, flag it. A wrong location kills user trust permanently.
3. NEVER use em dashes anywhere.
4. Photos must have verified open licenses with attribution recorded.
5. Money, ruled by Hidde 2026-07-27, replacing the earlier €50/month ceiling: **no spending without his explicit approval, ever.** Beyond that there is no fixed monthly ceiling, on the one condition he stated himself: the project has to start earning. Keep runs efficient anyway; waste needs no budget to be wrong.
6. The goal is revenue, not a beautiful product nobody buys. If Hidde starts adding features before validation, remind him: "Have we validated that real people will pay for this?"
7. NEVER ship a page (tree, question, city, or collection) that doesn't conform to SEO_GEO_BLUEPRINT.md. Changes to that document itself require Hidde's explicit approval and a version bump.
8. **Superlatives, loosened by Hidde 2026-08-13.** His words, on being told a pass had refused a superlative rather than risk contradicting another page: "wat maakt t uit als je het net fout hebt over dikte of hoogte... 80/20 is goed, liever wel online en af en toe een foutje dan zo spastisch over iets niets belangrijks. Het enige echt belangrijke is locatie." So: **write the superlative you believe, say where the number comes from, and move on.** Being wrong about a girth or a height is an edit, and a reader can tell us. Do not spend a paragraph hedging a measurement, and do not drop a good line to avoid a risk that costs nothing to fix.

    What survives, because it is not about precision: **two of our own pages must not claim the same crown.** "The largest fig in Palermo" on two different trees is not an imprecision, it is the site contradicting itself in one breath, and a reader who notices stops trusting both pages. That is now a script rather than a writing rule, `python3 scripts/superlatives.py`, which finds pages claiming the same title and says so. A writer no longer has to hold the whole corpus in their head, which is what made this rule expensive in the first place.

    The location rule is untouched and outranks all of it: a wrong pin is the one error a reader cannot forgive, because they are already standing in the wrong place before any correction reaches them.
9. Use one canonical common name per species so the species pages (Contract F) group correctly. When a species has multiple valid common names, pick the nationality-neutral one and match what other cities already use (e.g. Quercus robur is "Pedunculate Oak", never "English Oak"; Platanus x acerifolia is "London Plane"). The scientific name in parentheses is the tiebreaker for whether two trees are the same species.
10. **Publish public trees only, and never a location its source deliberately withholds.** Decided by Hidde on 2026-07-21. Some registers hide or blur the position of particular trees, and the Woodland Trust does this openly, because visitors damage ancient trees: soil compaction over the root plate, climbing, carving, and simple footfall. Others stand on private land whose owner never invited anyone. This site publishes exact coordinates and actively sends people to the trunk, which makes it capable of causing that harm rather than merely reporting it.

    So: if a source obscures a tree's location, treat that as a decision by people who know the tree and do not undo it, even where the position can be worked out from somewhere else. That half is absolute and is what makes this a question 3 rule in the sense of the mandate: it outranks goal 1, goal 2 and every argument about speed.

    **The access half is narrower than it was read, loosened by Hidde on 2026-08-13.** His question: "hoe erg is het nou als een boom op een schoolplein of kerk stad of zelfs bedrijfsterrein, vaak kun je die gewoon gaan bekijken toch." He is right, and the text always allowed it ("unless it is genuinely open to visitors"); what blocked those trees was the sentence that used to follow, "when in doubt, leave it out", applied to a doubt it was never written for. The rule was written about HARM, a tree damaged by visitors and a person's home invaded. A gate that might be shut is not harm, it is INCONVENIENCE, and the honest answer to inconvenience on this site has always been to say so rather than to hide the thing, exactly as `location_precision` does for a rough pin. So the deleted sentence is replaced by three tests, and a tree that passes all three ships:

    - **Can they get in without asking anyone's permission?** A churchyard, a cathedral close, a hospital garden, a campus, a company's grounds where the gate stands open: yes. A reception desk to talk past, a doorbell, a code, a guard who checks: no. Paid entry is a yes, not a no, as long as `access` says so.

      **A booking is a yes too, ruled by Hidde 2026-09-01**, on being shown two verified Menorca trees held back because the Illa del Llatzeret is reached only on a two-and-a-half-hour boat tour: "lijkt mij geen probleem als het maar duidelijk is dat je er niet zomaar komt." He is right and the earlier reading was this rule applied harder than it says. The "appointment" this list refuses is asking a PRIVATE party's permission to enter their space, the doorbell and the reception desk. A tour anyone can book, a ticketed opening time, a guided visit to a public site: those are the paid-entry case wearing a timetable, and the honest answer is the one this rule gives everywhere else, say plainly what the visitor will meet. The test that does not bend: is the thing between the reader and the tree a PRICE AND A SCHEDULE, which we can state, or somebody's PERMISSION, which we cannot promise? What this does not touch is harm: a location its source withholds, and somebody's home, both still absolute.
    - **Does the `access` field say honestly what they will meet?** "In the churchyard, open in daylight." "On the company's grounds; the gate is open on weekdays." "Visible from the road, the grounds themselves are closed." Where we cannot state it honestly, the tree waits as a lead. That is the whole cost of getting this wrong: somebody walks and finds a gate, tells us, and we fix a sentence.
    - **Is it not somebody's home?** Front gardens, courtyards of dwellings, private houses and their grounds stay out, whatever the register says and however fine the tree. This one does not bend, because the person we would be sending strangers to never asked for any of it.

    **A tree you can see but not reach is publishable, and says so (Hidde, 2026-08-13: "yes these ones we should use").** Hobart's Government House oak is the case that decided it: the register says it stands by the main entrance courtyard and is "highly visible as one approaches", which means somebody can stand on public ground and look at it. The promise is standing in front of something epic and old, and a wall between the visitor and the trunk does not break that promise as long as the page says the wall is there. So a view-only tree ships when three things hold: it is genuinely visible from ground the public may stand on, the pin marks the VIEWING PLACE rather than the trunk, and `access` says plainly what they will and will not be able to do ("visible from the road; the grounds are closed"). What it does not license is a tree you can only glimpse from a private neighbour's window, or one where "visible" is our guess rather than a source's statement.

    **Schools are the exception, and it is not about the tree (Hidde, 2026-08-13: "agree on the schools unless we find evidence").** A page saying "walk onto the schoolyard and look at the oak" sends adult strangers to where children are, during hours no register records. So a tree on school grounds ships only when there is real evidence the grounds are genuinely open (a public path crosses them, the park is public and the school merely borders it, the site is open at weekends and the access line says so). Absent that evidence it stays a lead. Bordeaux is the case to check rather than assume: 51 of its register trees carry a school site, several of them inside Parc du Bourran, which is a public park.

    **Churches are a yes, with one distinction.** A churchyard, a cathedral close, a temple or shrine precinct is public ground in practice and is where the best old trees stand, which is why Step 1 tells a pass to search them first. What is NOT the same thing is an enclosed convent or monastery garden, a cloister, or a religious institution now serving as a school or barracks: those are closed unless a source says otherwise, and several already sit blocked with exactly that evidence (Prague's Franciscan yew, whose own register calls it inaccessible; Perugia's holm oak inside an army language school).

    Prefer trees a city already signposts, because that is a public authority saying visitors are welcome.
