## Mingo Oak (Mingo County, WV) — REJECTED, dead
White oak, once the oldest and largest known living white oak in the world (age at death calculated at 577 years, sprouted c.1361), died from fumes off a nearby burning coal refuse pile and was felled 23 September 1938; ~2,000 people watched it fall. Confirmed by Wikipedia and West Virginia Public Broadcasting's history piece. No living successor or clone found in this pass. Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mingo_Oak, https://wvpublic.org/september-23-1938-cutting-of-the-mingo-oak/

## Liberty Tree (Boston) — REJECTED, dead
An elm on Boston Common (actually near Washington/Essex Streets, Boston's South End) under which the Sons of Liberty gathered before the American Revolution; felled by Loyalists in August 1775. Already established as dead in our own published Boston page (data/cities/boston.json FAQ: "No. The Liberty Tree ... was felled by Loyalists in August 1775. A plaque and a bas-relief mark the site ... no tree on this list claims its mantle."). A plaque/bas-relief marks the site, not a tree. No living successor claims direct lineage. Not delivered.

## Sacred Oak (Oley Township, PA) — REJECTED, private land, not genuinely open
Alive: a chinkapin oak (Quercus muehlenbergii), estimated 500-700+ years old (germination ~1420 +/-100), one of Pennsylvania's oldest documented trees, a Penn Charter tree predating William Penn. Girth 21ft2in (~645cm), height 73ft, spread 118ft, per Wikipedia and the International Oak Society. Lightning-damaged in 2001, restored with arborist care, alive and maintained as of a 2007 fertilization/deadwood pass. NOT DELIVERED on access grounds: per Wikipedia, the tree stands on private property and "is typically off limits to visitors," specifically because of documented pagan/ritual activity on site that led the owner to restrict access; no standing public right of entry. A "Sacred Oak Public Visitation" listing exists (reading.macaronikid.com), suggesting occasional scheduled open days by the owner's invitation, but that is not "get in without asking anyone's permission" and does not meet the access bar. Worth revisiting as a lead if a future pass finds the owner now runs regular open visitation. Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sacred_Oak, https://www.internationaloaksociety.org/content/sacred-oak-oley

## Pow-Wow Oak (Lowell, MA) — REJECTED, dead
White oak, c.1700 to 22 May 2013, standing on Clark Road in the Belvidere neighborhood of Lowell, Massachusetts (near the Tewksbury line), over 300 years old. Believed to be a Native American (Wamesit) pow-wow meeting site and tied to Revolutionary War history. A large upper branch broke off onto Clark Road on 21 May 2013 after a storm; the City of Lowell removed the whole tree the next day for safety. Confirmed by Wikipedia. This also resolves the location discrepancy in our lead: it stood in LOWELL, Massachusetts, not Cambridge. No living successor found. Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pow-Wow_Oak_Tree

## Old Treaty Elm (Chicago) — REJECTED, dead, marker only
American elm in Chicago's Sauganash neighborhood, marked the boundary of the Fort Dearborn Reservation and the Old Indian Boundary Line. In poor health by the late 1920s, given tree surgery in March 1929, and died by 1934. What our own lead entry actually shows is the 1937 HISTORICAL MARKER, not a tree, and the marker itself stands several feet west of where the trunk actually was (in what is now the middle of Kilbourn Avenue, just north of Rogers Avenue). No tree exists at this site. Confirmed by Wikipedia. Not delivered. Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Treaty_Elm

## Tiadaghton Elm (Pine Creek, Clinton County, PA) — REJECTED, dead
An elm along the west bank of Pine Creek, traditionally the site where the "Fair Play Men" declared independence from Britain on 4 July 1776 (the "Pine Creek Declaration of Independence"). Last blossomed in 1973, cut down in 1975 after disease and age. Confirmed by two independent sources (a WebSearch multi-source summary citing pabucketlist.com/uncoveringpa.com, and susquehannavalley.blogspot.com's 2022 visit post). No genuine living successor: a large tree stands near the historical marker today but multiple sources are explicit that it is NOT the original tree and not a planted descendant, just an unrelated tree that happens to be nearby -- does not meet our replacement-tree bar. Additional flag found in this pass: when part of the original trunk was examined by the Lycoming County Historical Society in 1988, tree-ring counts showed it was roughly 40 years too young to have stood in 1776, and the site itself was reportedly chosen without real historical basis by a folklorist in 1918 -- so even the underlying story is doubtful. Not delivered. Source: https://susquehannavalley.blogspot.com/2022/03/the-tiadaghton-elm-historical-marker.html

## Three Mile Oak (Parole/Annapolis, MD) — REJECTED, dead, no confirmed living successor
White oak, roughly six feet in diameter, that stood three miles from the Maryland State House and marked where George Washington passed on 19 December 1783 en route to resign his commission, and where Lafayette passed in 1824. Struck by lightning, went hollow, damaged by fire, and blew down 22 May 1909. Confirmed by Wikipedia and the HMdb marker page. The stump/concrete-block memorial was relocated about 500 feet in 1967 (now near the US 50 cloverleaf, across from where Famous Dave's/Red Lobster stood, close to the old Toys R Us at Annapolis Mall). Wikipedia's article states a new oak was 'planted in 1967 near the relocated marker', but this could not be confirmed: a directly-fetched, more detailed local blog post (patch.com, a firsthand description of the site) describes only 'evergreen boughs' beside the plaque and the original concrete stump-block with twisted metal bars, with no mention of any oak tree at the site at all. Given the conflict between an unconfirmed Wikipedia line and a directly-fetched source that describes the site without a tree, and given our own lead's photos are all of the concrete marker block (no tree visible in any of them), this is not delivered. Worth a very targeted future check (e.g. satellite imagery of the site, or a fresh site visit report) if someone wants to settle whether an oak was ever actually planted there and survives. Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Three_Mile_Oak, https://patch.com/maryland/annapolis/bp--blog-three-mile-oak-tree-in-parole
