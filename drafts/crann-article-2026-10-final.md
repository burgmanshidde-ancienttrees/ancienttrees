Draft article: Worth a detour

A Dutch tree fan on mapping old trees, and how you can help.

It started in Palermo. Frustrated at not finding a place to park, I ended up leaving the car on a side street, and when I got out, I was struck by the immense tree I had parked under. It was a giant fig (not that I knew the species at the time). I was just amazed by what I was looking at: how big it was, how long it must have stood there, and what it had seen in all the years before I came along.

I had felt the same in Lisbon, where work took me for several years. Strolling through the parks between meetings, I found old jacarandas and fig trees that seemed ages old, definitely planted by someone no longer living, for us to marvel at. These trees slowly became an addition to my trips, and so I decided to build an app to find them.

That app is Ancient Trees. It allows you to find, walk and collect remarkable old trees wherever you are. I'm not a tree expert, just a big fan, and I'm trying to share that passion. The app shows nearly 4,000 of them in more than 230 places, and the list keeps growing, much of it thanks to people who add their local favourite.

The bar for every tree is the one that fig set: is it worth a detour? Big or old for its kind, an interesting story, or something you have to see with your own eyes.

These three Irish trees are at the top of my list for my next visit.

The first is the Hungry Tree at King's Inns in Dublin, a London plane that grew against a cast iron bench and has been swallowing it for decades. The Tree Council of Ireland calls it an otherwise unremarkable specimen, and yet it became one of Dublin's most photographed trees. Through the archway from Henrietta Street, its bark spills over the arms of the seat.
[ancienttrees.app/dublin/hungry-tree]

The second is the Witch's Yew in the Rock Close at Blarney Castle, growing straight out of the limestone. It is thought to be more than 600 years old, roughly as old as the castle keep, and legend says the Blarney Witch lives there.
[ancienttrees.app/cork/witchs-yew]

The third is the King Oak at Charleville, near Tullamore. Its lower branches spread up to 27 metres and are propped up to keep them from falling. Superstition says that when it drops a limb, a member of the Bury family, the estate's long-time owners, will die. In 1963 lightning split the trunk, and weeks later Colonel Charles Howard-Bury, the last of the line, was dead.
[ancienttrees.app/tullamore/king-oak]

They are three of the 31 Irish trees on the map today, and I'm looking for help to grow it. You can add trees you like, take a photo with the app when you pass one (Easiest with the app), improve the trees we already have, or add the stories that make them unique. Each tree has a story that only locals know. Some of the best trees on the map were found by readers: a fig in Tangier, a plane tree in Paris. In Lisbon, a local tree group walked with the app and added four trees and their photos in one afternoon.

If you really want to help, you can become a city ambassador and help guide others to see the most remarkable trees in your town.

Ancient Trees may be a digital app, but in the end it is made to get people outside and enjoy nature. You'll find it at ancienttrees.app, and you can reach me at info@ancienttrees.app.
