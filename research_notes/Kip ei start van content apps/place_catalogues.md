# How iNaturalist, Geocaching.com and Atlas Obscura got their first entries and contributors

Scope: iNaturalist 2008 to 2014, Geocaching 2000 to 2005, Atlas Obscura 2009 to 2014. Early years only. Facts carry a source; inference is marked as such. Research date: 2026-10-23.

A note on sources. The best first-person accounts for iNaturalist (the "iNat Turns 10" Q&A with Ken-ichi Ueda on inaturalist.org, and the "History of iNaturalist" post on inaturalist.nz) sit behind a Cloudflare bot-verification page that could not be opened by any tool here; I did not try to bypass it. The 2010 CNN piece on Atlas Obscura returned HTTP 451 (geo-blocked). The GPSgames.org "History of Geocaching" (the independent, critical account) refused the connection. Those three gaps are flagged below where they matter. Everything else is from pages that were actually read.

## Key question 1: Who wrote the first entries, and how many existed when the public arrived?

### Takeaway
All three catalogues were seeded by hand by the founders and a circle of friends, not by an import: Atlas Obscura with "the first hundred or so" places, Geocaching.com with 75 caches typed in from e-mails, iNaturalist with the founder's own observations (observation 2 is a bulrush growing in his office). None of them opened to the public with a full catalogue; they opened nearly empty and asked.

### Cited Findings

**iNaturalist**
- Ken-ichi Ueda conceived the idea in 2005; the site launched in 2008 as the Master's final project of Ueda, Nate Agrin and Jessica Kline at UC Berkeley's School of Information. — [iNaturalist About page](https://www.inaturalist.org/pages/about.html); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/INaturalist)
- Internet debut was March 2008. — [iNat Turns 10 (mirror, only the summary was readable)](https://inaturalist.ca/posts/39714-inat-turns-10-2018-03-24)
- The lowest-numbered record still on the site is observation 2: a California bulrush in Berkeley, observed 20 March 2008 by Ken-ichi Ueda, with his note "Growing in our office. Maybe this doesn't count since it's captive…". Observation 1 no longer exists, probably deleted as a test. Some records carry 2007 observation dates despite higher IDs; forum users attribute these to data merged in from other systems (a New Zealand database is suggested). — [iNaturalist Forum, "First records submitted to iNat"](https://forum.inaturalist.org/t/first-records-submitted-to-inat/70413)
- After graduation Agrin and Ueda continued the site with web developer Sean McGregor. — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/INaturalist)
- By November 2014, the catalogue held just over one million observations of 50,000+ species from a community of 60,000+ naturalists, with a new observation every 45 seconds; "nearly half" were Research Grade and fed into GBIF. — [California Academy of Sciences press release, 10 Nov 2014](https://www.calacademy.org/press/releases/citizen-science-network-inaturalist-celebrates-millionth-nature-observation-as-it)

**Geocaching**
- 2 May 2000: GPS selective availability was switched off, improving civilian accuracy about tenfold. 3 May 2000: Dave Ulmer hid a black bucket near Beavercreek, Oregon and posted the coordinates to the Usenet group sci.geo.satellite-nav under the name "Great American GPS Stash Hunt", with the rule "Take some stuff, leave some stuff". Two readers found it within three days. — [Geocaching.com history page](https://www.geocaching.com/about/history.aspx); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geocaching)
- Within the first month Mike Teague collected coordinates from around the world on his personal home page and a "GPS Stash Hunt" mailing list was created; Matt Stum coined "geocaching" on that list on 30 May 2000 (members found "stash" had negative connotations). — [Geocaching.com history](https://www.geocaching.com/about/history.aspx); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geocaching)
- Jeremy Irish found Teague's page in July 2000, bought a GPS for about $115, and announced Geocaching.com on 2 September 2000 after Teague's site went down; it launched with 75 caches, entered by hand from e-mailed submissions, and the earliest caches have no usernames attached. — [Inside Geocaching HQ podcast transcript, ep. 25 (Irish)](https://www.geocaching.com/blog/?p=66865); [Geocaching.com history](https://www.geocaching.com/about/history.aspx)
- The Oregon Encyclopedia phrases the September 2000 figure as "75 active participants" rather than caches; the company's own sources say 75 caches. The two readings are not reconciled. — [Oregon Encyclopedia, "Geocaching"](https://www.oregonencyclopedia.org/articles/geocaching/)
- By the end of 2000: almost 300 caches in Oregon, more than 300 in Washington, "nearly 10,000 in 86 countries" (the 10,000 figure may count placements or participants loosely; it is a tertiary source citing geocaching.com). — [Oregon Encyclopedia](https://www.oregonencyclopedia.org/articles/geocaching/)
- Irish says he wanted a bigger community so there would be more caches to find, "since he couldn't hide and find them all himself." — [Irish podcast transcript](https://www.geocaching.com/blog/?p=66865)

**Atlas Obscura**
- Foer and Thuras met in 2007 (through a society meeting about Athanasius Kircher), hired a web designer in 2008, launched in 2009; Annetta Black was the first senior editor. — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atlas_Obscura)
- Per early employee Seth Teicher, co-founders Foer and Thuras "added the first hundred or so places themselves", with their circle of friends contributing too; new visitors saw the existing entries as examples and were encouraged to add their own. — [Get Together case study, "The National Geographic of the digital age"](https://gettogether.substack.com/p/the-national-geographic-of-the-digital)
- Thuras: "It totally started just as a, kind of, a passion project"; they "pulled together a tiny amount of money from friends and family and built the website." — [NPS symposium interview with Dylan Thuras](https://www.nps.gov/articles/000/symposium-interview-with-dylan-thuras-are-we-there-yet-preserving-roadside-architecture-and-attractions.htm)
- The founders started "by collecting unusual places they had found while traveling"; they deliberately did not make "an online book" and built it on a wiki-style model. — [Urban Omnibus, 14 Mar 2012](https://urbanomnibus.net/2012/03/atlas-obscura/)
- At the June 2009 public launch Foer listed thirteen example places "recently added" (synchronous fireflies in the Smokies, the Darvaza crater, Sedlec ossuary, the Icelandic Phallological Museum, Galileo's finger, La Gomera's whistled language, etc.), i.e. the catalogue was a short list of greatest-hits oddities the founders already knew. — [Boing Boing, "Introducing the Atlas Obscura", 15 Jun 2009](https://boingboing.net/2009/06/15/introducing-the-atla.html)
- Cumulative total a decade later: contributors had shared "more than 14,000 entries" over its first decade. — [Dallas Museum of Art event listing](https://cms.dma.org/node/14130)

### Inferences
- In all three cases the founder's own hobby output was the seed, and the seed was tiny (dozens to about a hundred items). Nobody imported a register. (Inference from the three seed counts above.)
- iNaturalist's seed was the slowest: a research tool used by its makers and their circle for roughly two to three years before any growth push (inference from the 2008 launch, the "mostly California" description, and Loarie only joining in 2010/2011; see Q2).

### Gaps
- No source gives the number of observations or registered users iNaturalist had in 2008, 2009, 2010 or 2011. The forum thread explicitly asks whether anyone outside staff was active in the first year and nobody answers it.
- No source gives the number of Atlas Obscura places at launch beyond "the first hundred or so" (Teicher) or at any point 2010 to 2014.
- Worldwide geocache totals for 2001 to 2004 were not found in a primary source; see Q4.

## Key question 2: How did the first contributors come?

### Takeaway
Each project rode an existing community it did not create: Geocaching on a Usenet GPS newsgroup plus a Slashdot and New York Times wave inside its first two months; Atlas Obscura on Boing Boing's readership (the founders guest-blogged the launch) and then on a physical event, Obscura Day, amplified by National Geographic; iNaturalist on the Bay Area naturalist and science scene, then on institutional BioBlitz events and a merger with a museum.

### Cited Findings

**Geocaching (2000 to 2001)**
- The seed community was the sci.geo.satellite-nav Usenet group and the GPS Stash Hunt mailing list; Irish himself arrived through "the newsgroup, then Mike Teague's home page". — [Geocaching.com history](https://www.geocaching.com/about/history.aspx); [Irish transcript](https://www.geocaching.com/blog/?p=66865)
- Teague "passed leadership" to Irish in September 2000, so the existing community was redirected to the new site rather than recruited from zero. — [Geocaching.com history](https://www.geocaching.com/about/history.aspx)
- Press came within weeks: Slashdot on 25 September 2000 (the traffic took down Irish's bedroom server on a low-bandwidth line), the New York Times "Circuits" section in October 2000, CNN in December 2000; the company's own page says people joined "through word of mouth, press coverage, and accidental discoveries." — [Geocaching.com history](https://www.geocaching.com/about/history.aspx); [Irish transcript](https://www.geocaching.com/blog/?p=66865)
- The community tools were mailing lists and a forum (no forum launch date given). — [Irish transcript](https://www.geocaching.com/blog/?p=66865)
- Physical gatherings began early: first Event Cache 24 March 2001 in Austin, Texas; first Travel Bug released 30 August 2001; first CITO (Cache In Trash Out) event 26 April 2003; EarthCache launched 2004 with the Geological Society of America (first one in New South Wales, January 2004). — [Geocaching HQ milestones post](https://www.geocaching.com/blog/?p=147963); [milestones tag](https://www.geocaching.com/blog/tag/milestones/)
- Regional state-level communities grew their own forums (e.g. oregongeocaching.com). — [Oregon Encyclopedia](https://www.oregonencyclopedia.org/articles/geocaching/)
- Regional pace, anecdotal: British Columbia took February 2001 to August 2002 to reach 300 caches (a cache-owner's page); Maryland had roughly 100 caches in early 2002 (forum recollection). — [GC1V4YH cache page](https://www.geocaching.com/geocache/GC1V4YH); [mdgps.org forum](https://www.mdgps.org/forum/viewtopic.php?p=114963)

**Atlas Obscura (2009 to 2012)**
- Launch channel: a week of guest-blogging on Boing Boing starting 15 June 2009, with the explicit ask "We're counting on you, Boing Boing readers, to help us fill out the map", and the admission that "the site is still very much in beta". — [Boing Boing](https://boingboing.net/2009/06/15/introducing-the-atla.html)
- First year: "essentially a wiki", about 200,000 unique visitors a month "without paid promotion"; Teicher: "a lot of people came back. From the very beginning, our time on site was really, really high." — [Get Together case study](https://gettogether.substack.com/p/the-national-geographic-of-the-digital)
- First Obscura Day, 20 March 2010: events were lined up in 27 cities before the day (Atlas Obscura's own announcement); about 4,000 people attended 80 events in 20 countries, National Geographic promoted it, and attendees spread it on social media, blogs, Flickr and YouTube; the case study's thesis is that Atlas "transformed from a mere wiki into a community because of" this event. Teicher (who produced it) said the main goal was to make the site's community feel real beyond the screen. — [Atlas Obscura announcement](https://www.atlasobscura.com/articles/clear-your-calendar-and-save-the-date-international-obscura-day-is-march-20-2010); [Make: coverage](https://makezine.com/article/home/obscura-day-march-20-2010/); [Get Together](https://gettogether.substack.com/p/the-national-geographic-of-the-digital)
- Thuras on the purpose of Obscura Day: "Creating a real-world community who are engaging with us, each other and these places." — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atlas_Obscura)
- 2011 edition: "over a hundred events around the world"; 2012 (28 April) was the third annual, in "more than 100 cities". — [Urban Omnibus 2012](https://urbanomnibus.net/2012/03/atlas-obscura/); [Mental Floss 2012](https://mentalfloss.com/article/30468/obscura-day-2012-get-out-there-and-explore)
- 2010 press and media: CNN (19 March 2010, the day before Obscura Day; described it as a Wikipedia-style user-generated site with volunteer editors; page geo-blocked here), KQED Forum (2010, around the first book), Steve Korver interview (July 2010). — [CNN](https://www.cnn.com/2010/TRAVEL/03/19/travel.atlas.obscura/); [KQED](https://www.kqed.org/forum/2010101857535/atlas-obscura-maps-the-hidden-wonders-of-the-world); [Korver](https://stevekorver.com/atlas-obscura/)
- Local chapters came later: Atlas Obscura Societies in seven cities (New York, Philadelphia, Washington D.C., Chicago, Denver, Los Angeles, Seattle), with Wikipedia saying nine by 2021. — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atlas_Obscura)
- Who the audience was (2012, Thuras): mainly people who already explore on their own, plus younger "explore-y" visitors and older history and engineering enthusiasts at the field trips. — [Urban Omnibus](https://urbanomnibus.net/2012/03/atlas-obscura/)

**iNaturalist (2008 to 2014)**
- The earliest known non-staff member is Clinton Duffy ("clinton", a New Zealand shark scientist by username), on the site since May 2008, "to my knowledge" the first non-staff active member. — [iNaturalist Forum](https://forum.inaturalist.org/t/first-records-submitted-to-inat/70413)
- The 10-year retrospective describes the arc as "from a website used mostly in California, to a platform with international portals, multiple languages, mobile apps for iOS and Android." — [iNat Turns 10 (via search summary)](https://www.inaturalist.org/blog/14984-inat-turns-10)
- Scott Loarie (then a Stanford research fellow and Berkeley lecturer) began collaborating with Ueda in 2010 (About page) or 2011 (Wikipedia, Bay Nature); they formed iNaturalist, LLC in 2011 and "began growing the community and expanding the platform, including launching the first mobile app." Ueda in a 2019 interview called Loarie the one "who brought the party". — [About page](https://www.inaturalist.org/pages/about.html); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/INaturalist); [Bay Nature 2026](https://baynature.org/magazine/summer2026/the-many-faces-of-inaturalist/)
- Events as the growth engine: in 2011 iNaturalist powered the Global Amphibian BioBlitz and Global Reptile BioBlitz (San Francisco Chronicle coverage cited). — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/INaturalist); [Berkeley I School](https://www.ischool.berkeley.edu/news/2022/new-york-times-profiles-app-inaturalist-created-mims-alumni)
- Institutional partnerships: the California Academy of Sciences began working with iNaturalist in 2011; Mexico's CONABIO adapted the platform as NaturaLista from 2012; New Zealand's Bio-Recording Network Trust ran NatureWatchNZ; CREAF (Spain) wrote much of the 2014 map code. — [Cal Academy press release, Nov 2014](https://www.calacademy.org/press/releases/citizen-science-network-inaturalist-celebrates-millionth-nature-observation-as-it)
- Mobile apps: Google Play listing dated 4 June 2013, iTunes 25 June 2013 (these are store listing dates cited on Wikipedia; the About page says the first app came after 2011). — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/INaturalist)
- Merger: iNaturalist became an initiative of the California Academy of Sciences on 24 April 2014 (press release 14 May 2014); Loarie's staff and office moved to the Academy. The May 2014 National Geographic piece "San Francisco's Parks Scoured in Wildlife Inventory" is cited for that year's BioBlitz activity. — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/INaturalist); [Bay Nature 2023](https://baynature.org/2023/09/12/inaturalist-strikes-out-on-its-own/)
- Growth shape: "Since 2012, the number of participants and observations has roughly doubled each year"; 1 million observations reached in November 2014. — [About page](https://www.inaturalist.org/pages/about.html); [Cal Academy press release](https://www.calacademy.org/press/releases/citizen-science-network-inaturalist-celebrates-millionth-nature-observation-as-it)

### Inferences
- Geocaching's first thousand contributors arrived in roughly four months (September to December 2000) because three things coincided: a pre-existing newsgroup with the right gear, a handoff from the previous hub, and tech press (Slashdot, NYT, CNN) in quick succession. The product did not have to create demand; it had to catch it. (Inference from the dated press list and the end-2000 counts.)
- Atlas Obscura's distinctive move was turning a wiki into a calendar: the first thing it did after launch was a physical day with hosts in 27 cities, and the growth numbers it quotes afterwards are event counts, not entry counts. (Inference from the Obscura Day figures and the absence of any published entry counts for 2010 to 2014.)
- iNaturalist did not grow on prompts to the general public; it grew by attaching to organised counting events (BioBlitzes) and institutions that already had naturalists, then by becoming part of a museum. The "doubling since 2012" dates the inflection to the year after the LLC, the first BioBlitz partnerships and the Cal Academy collaboration began. (Inference from the dated partnerships and the About-page growth sentence.)

### Gaps
- No source names the channel through which iNaturalist's first few hundred non-staff users arrived in 2008 to 2010 (Berkeley circle, naturalist listservs, Flickr groups, or press). The "iNat Turns 10" Q&A with Ueda almost certainly covers this and could not be read.
- No source gives the share of 2010 to 2012 iNaturalist observations that came through BioBlitz events versus organic sign-ups.
- The size of the Boing Boing effect on Atlas Obscura's contributor count is not documented anywhere found; only the visitor figure (200,000 a month in year one) is.

## Key question 3: What made a visitor become a contributor?

### Takeaway
The documented converters are: a visible, low-friction "add a place / edit anyone's entry" wiki affordance with the founders' examples as templates (Atlas Obscura); a game loop where contribution is the hobby itself (hiding a cache is how you play, and finding one means logging it) plus a distance-sorted list from your own location (Geocaching); and, for iNaturalist, a social identification layer where other people confirm or improve your identification and you cannot simply mark someone wrong. Badges and ambassador models do not appear in the early-years record of any of the three.

### Cited Findings

**Atlas Obscura**
- Launch model: "Anyone can enter new places into the Atlas Obscura, or edit content that someone else has already contributed." — [Boing Boing 2009](https://boingboing.net/2009/06/15/introducing-the-atla.html)
- New visitors saw the founders' entries as examples and were encouraged to add their own. — [Get Together](https://gettogether.substack.com/p/the-national-geographic-of-the-digital)
- By March 2010 submissions went to "a panel of volunteer editors who vetted and published the pieces" (CNN, via search summary; page geo-blocked). By July 2010: "all are welcome to contribute, but the listings are edited and fact checked"; Thuras: "We love these places and want to respect and honour them." — [CNN 2010](https://www.cnn.com/2010/TRAVEL/03/19/travel.atlas.obscura/); [Korver 2010](https://stevekorver.com/atlas-obscura/)
- By 2012: users submit "everything from short tips to long articles"; editorial staff checks facts, verifies submissions and sometimes writes entries. Thuras (later): "people submit locations to the Atlas and we edit them and add them." The founders "hired editors and fact checkers as submissions grew." — [Urban Omnibus 2012](https://urbanomnibus.net/2012/03/atlas-obscura/); [NPS interview](https://www.nps.gov/articles/000/symposium-interview-with-dylan-thuras-are-we-there-yet-preserving-roadside-architecture-and-attractions.htm); [Get Together](https://gettogether.substack.com/p/the-national-geographic-of-the-digital)
- The social layer was offline: Obscura Day hosts ran "expeditions, back-room tours, and hidden treasures in your own hometown"; attendees posted the day on Flickr and YouTube. — [Get Together](https://gettogether.substack.com/p/the-national-geographic-of-the-digital)
- A planned 2012 relaunch would add "looser thematic groupings and more editorial content", i.e. a shift from pure wiki toward editorial. — [Urban Omnibus](https://urbanomnibus.net/2012/03/atlas-obscura/)

**Geocaching**
- Irish built distance sorting from the start: he "researched great-circle distance calculations to sort caches by distance from the user." — [Irish transcript](https://www.geocaching.com/blog/?p=66865)
- The first rule of the game was itself a contribution rule: "Take some stuff, leave some stuff." — [Geocaching.com history](https://www.geocaching.com/about/history.aspx)
- Each cache holds a logbook where finders record date and time; players are "encouraged to post their geocaching adventure photos and stories" online. — [Oregon Encyclopedia](https://www.oregonencyclopedia.org/articles/geocaching/)
- Trackables (Travel Bugs from 30 August 2001, geocoins from 20 September 2001) gave people a reason to return to the site between finds; early Travel Bug tags were made by hand with a dog-tag stamper. — [Milestones](https://www.geocaching.com/blog/?p=147963); [Irish transcript](https://www.geocaching.com/blog/?p=66865)
- Three community guidelines from the start: no trespassing or illegal activity, family-friendly content, no solicitation or commercialization. — [Irish transcript](https://www.geocaching.com/blog/?p=66865)
- The site was designed to look like an outdoors site: "loosely based on Ansel Adams' website, using color imagery and outdoor photos." — [Irish transcript](https://www.geocaching.com/blog/?p=66865)
- Premium Membership launched 4 March 2002; first-year joiners are called "Charter members" (a status label that still exists). — [Milestones](https://www.geocaching.com/blog/?p=147963)

**iNaturalist**
- The platform is built on "crowdsourced observations and identifications, where users confirm or improve each other's IDs." Ueda on the design: the evidence-based approach is supported because "users can't just mark identifications as incorrect" (NYT 2022, paraphrased). — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/INaturalist); [Berkeley I School](https://www.ischool.berkeley.edu/news/2022/new-york-times-profiles-app-inaturalist-created-mims-alumni)
- Ueda's stated motive was social, not instrumental: the site "grew out of the belief that he might not be alone in his desire to combine nature and the Web." — [SciStarter](https://scistarter.org/sips-with-scientists-ken-ichi-ueda-co-founder-of-i)
- Observations earned "Research Grade" status once the community agreed on the identification, and that status meant export to GBIF (nearly half of the first million). — [Cal Academy press release](https://www.calacademy.org/press/releases/citizen-science-network-inaturalist-celebrates-millionth-nature-observation-as-it)
- Loarie and Ueda "saw iNaturalist's purpose differently from the start" (Ueda the social network, Loarie the scientific data engine; the Bay Nature piece frames it this way). — [Bay Nature 2026](https://baynature.org/magazine/summer2026/the-many-faces-of-inaturalist/)
- Automated species suggestions did not exist in this period (first released 2017). — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/INaturalist)

### Inferences
- In none of the three was a badge or points system the early converter. Geocaching's "Charter member" is the only status label found before 2005, and it is a paid-membership label. (Inference from absence in all read sources; gamification may simply be undocumented.)
- The strongest converter documented is reciprocity with a human on the other side: someone identifies your bug, someone finds your cache and signs the log, an editor publishes your place. (Inference from the mechanics above.)
- Atlas Obscura's shift from "anyone can edit anyone's entry" (2009) to "submit, we edit and publish" (2010 to 2012) is the clearest documented instance of a wiki model being retired for a submission-plus-editor model within three years. (Inference from the dated descriptions.)

### Gaps
- No source describes the on-page prompt (copy, placement) that iNaturalist or Atlas Obscura used to turn a reader into a contributor.
- Nothing found on an ambassador or local-chapter programme at any of the three before 2014 except Obscura Day hosts (whose selection process is not described) and Atlas Obscura Societies (date of first Society not found).

## Key question 4: Super-contributors versus many light ones in the first years

### Takeaway
Atlas Obscura is explicit that a handful of near-daily editors carried the early catalogue and that it is still "fueled by a community of super contributors" ten years on; Geocaching's and iNaturalist's early contributor distributions are not documented in anything read, though iNaturalist's 2018 figures (300,000 observers, 50,000 identifiers) show the identifier role as the scarce one.

### Cited Findings
- Atlas Obscura, year one: "A handful of users visited almost daily to create and edit entries"; "ten years since its launch, Atlas remains fueled by a community of super contributors." — [Get Together](https://gettogether.substack.com/p/the-national-geographic-of-the-digital)
- iNaturalist, August 2018: 12 million verifiable observations, 170,000 species, "more than 300,000 observers and nearly 50,000 identifiers" (outside the period but the only contributor-role split found). — [Moore Foundation interview, Aug 2018](https://www.moore.org/article-detail?newsUrlName=beyond-the-lab-scott-loarie-ph.d.-and-ken-ichi-ueda)
- Geocaching: Mike Teague (first finder, first aggregator) and Dave Ulmer were the super-contributors of the pre-site months; Irish entered the first 75 caches himself. Named early placers of milestone items: Moun10Bike (second geocoin, 20 September 2001). — [Geocaching.com history](https://www.geocaching.com/about/history.aspx); [Milestones](https://www.geocaching.com/blog/?p=147963)
- Geocaching regional growth was slow and local: 300 caches in British Columbia took 18 months (February 2001 to August 2002). — [GC1V4YH](https://www.geocaching.com/geocache/GC1V4YH)

### Inferences
- For all three the first year's content was produced by under ten people (founders plus a "handful"), and the public's job was first to consume and come back (Teicher's high time-on-site), only later to add. (Inference from Q1 seed counts and the "handful" statement.)

### Gaps
- No Gini-style or top-10-percent figures for any of the three in the early years.
- No 2008 to 2014 figure for how many iNaturalist users had ever posted more than one observation.

## Key question 5: What did they try that failed?

### Takeaway
Documented failures are mostly operational (Geocaching's server died under Slashdot; Travel Bug tracking was done by hand and peeled stickers) and strategic (Atlas Obscura's open-wiki editing was replaced by editors; Geocaching's early commercialisation moves drew a rival listing site and a lasting "monopoly" complaint). Nobody found writes candidly about a growth tactic that simply did not work.

### Cited Findings
- Geocaching: the September 2000 Slashdot post took the site down (bedroom server, low bandwidth). Early Travel Bug tracking was "disorganized at first, with tracking codes peeled from stickers and matched to a list by hand." Irish wrote to his partners "around 2001 doubting the site would ever make money", and said it needed to sustain itself. — [Irish transcript](https://www.geocaching.com/blog/?p=66865)
- Geocaching funding: the company (Grounded Inc., later Groundspeak, founded 3 November 2000) was first funded by selling 144 t-shirts; Irish kept a day job; Premium Membership (4 March 2002) eventually funded full-time work for Irish and Alvord; Bryan Roth only went full-time in late 2005. — [Geocaching.com history](https://www.geocaching.com/about/history.aspx); [Milestones](https://www.geocaching.com/blog/?p=147963)
- Commercialisation backlash: "an independent accounting of the early history documents several controversial actions taken by Jeremy Irish and Grounded, Inc. ... aimed at increasing commercialization and monopolistic control over the hobby" (GPSgames.org history, cited by Wikipedia; the GPSgames page itself could not be opened). Navicache.com was created in February 2001 "as a less restrictive alternative to Geocaching.com, since it did not require physical containers or charge for listings" (Know Your Meme, a weak source). — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geocaching); [Know Your Meme](https://knowyourmeme.com/memes/subcultures/geocaching)
- Atlas Obscura: the open-edit wiki of June 2009 became a submit-to-volunteer-editors model by March 2010 and a staffed editorial model by 2012, with a 2012 relaunch toward "more editorial content." — [Boing Boing](https://boingboing.net/2009/06/15/introducing-the-atla.html); [CNN 2010](https://www.cnn.com/2010/TRAVEL/03/19/travel.atlas.obscura/); [Urban Omnibus](https://urbanomnibus.net/2012/03/atlas-obscura/)
- Atlas Obscura: no outside money for the first six years; the first major round ($2M, including The New York Times) came in 2015, i.e. the community phase was run on friends-and-family money and event revenue (Obscura Day "showed the team that it could charge for events and win sponsorship"). — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atlas_Obscura); [Get Together](https://gettogether.substack.com/p/the-national-geographic-of-the-digital)
- iNaturalist: the site ran three years as a side project (2008 to 2011) before an LLC, and six before an institutional home; the "doubling each year" is dated from 2012, which implies flat or slow growth in 2008 to 2011. — [About page](https://www.inaturalist.org/pages/about.html); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/INaturalist)

### Inferences
- The one tactic all three abandoned is "anyone edits anything": Geocaching never had it (caches were owner-listed), Atlas Obscura dropped it within a year, iNaturalist restricted it by design (you add an identification, you cannot delete someone else's). (Inference from Q3 findings.)
- Geocaching's growth outran its ability to serve it (server, hand-made tags), which is the opposite failure mode from iNaturalist's slow first three years. (Inference.)

### Gaps
- No founder of any of the three is on record, in anything read, naming a specific acquisition channel that failed (a press push that flopped, a partnership that produced nothing). Ueda's "iNat Turns 10" Q&A and the inaturalist.nz history post are the most likely places for this and were unreadable (Cloudflare verification).
- The GPSgames.org account of Groundspeak's early moves (listing exclusivity, data ownership, the premium wall) was not readable; its specific allegations are therefore not reproduced here beyond Wikipedia's one-sentence summary.
- No contemporaneous 2010 to 2014 statement from Atlas Obscura on why open editing was retired.
